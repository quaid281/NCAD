\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised anomaly detection in multivariate telemetry requires setting operational thresholds automatically via statistical calibration, such as Extreme Value Theory (EVT/SPOT), without ground-truth labels. While reconstructive baselines like TimesNet and TranAD achieve competitive ranking (PR-AUC $0.29\text{--}0.34$), heavy-tailed observation residuals cause severe false-alarm flooding, yielding nominal false-positive rates of $24\text{--}30\%$. We investigate whether forecasting in latent representation space via Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) conditions residuals for automated thresholding. Across 33 continuous telemetry streams from seven physical domains evaluated under held-out calibration (up to five seeds on the multi-seed subset), unconstrained latent prediction (TS-JEPA) suppresses nominal false-positive rates roughly four-fold down to $7.6\%$ (paired difference $-0.150$ against TimesNet, $p=0.007$). However, overall Point-F1 ($0.234$ vs. $0.197$, $p=0.54$) and PR-AUC ($0.323$ vs. $0.329$, $p=0.39$) remain statistically indistinguishable from baselines, while detection latency increases relative to the fastest reconstructive baseline ($\Delta t = 80$ vs. $48$ steps) and causal event recall drops ($10.7\%$ vs. $32.9\%$). Furthermore, evaluating three candidate physics-inspired regularizers (Koopman spectral entropy, Reynolds stress, and conservative potential flow) reveals domain-specific trade-offs rather than universal gains: linear Koopman operators excel on spacecraft telemetry (PR-AUC $0.591$ on SMAP), but curl-free priors fail on cyclic kinematics, and on aggregate, physical regularizers do not outperform unconstrained latent prediction ($p \ge 0.50$). Finally, EVT calibration systematically misses its nominal design risk ($q=10^{-3}$) on non-stationary telemetry, performing comparably to empirical percentiles. Latent prediction thus provides a more conservative detector that suppresses false alarms, but does not improve ranking fidelity or resolve tail calibration drift.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous anomaly detection in multi-sensor telemetry monitors operational health across aerospace systems, Earth observation, cyber-physical infrastructure, and wearable digital healthcare \cite{meng2019spacecraft, chalapathy2019deep}. In remote sensing deployments (such as the NASA Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) satellite, Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) rovers, industrial IoT facilities, and wearable biometric sensor arrays), dozens to hundreds of continuous telemetry channels concurrently track electrical power buses, thermal gradients, mechanical vibrations, and kinematics \cite{Hundman2018b}. When sensor faults or operational breakdowns emerge at the edge, monitoring systems must identify dynamical anomalies before hardware damage occurs \cite{cuellar2024explainable}.

\subsection{Challenges in Modeling Multi-Sensor Telemetry}
From a physical perspective, multi-sensor telemetry reflects the state trajectory of an underlying continuous physical system $\dot{x}(t) = \mathbf{f}(x(t))$. However, attempting to model dynamical equations directly in raw observation space faces four core challenges on edge devices. First, estimating instantaneous time derivatives $\dot{x} \approx \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ directly on discrete, noise-corrupted sensor streams amplifies high-frequency measurement noise, destabilizing numerical trajectory tracking. Second, remote sensing telemetry captures only a noisy surface projection of unmeasured thermodynamic, electrical, and environmental states, violating full-state observability. Third, in large sensor arrays ($K \ge 25$, such as NASA SMAP with $K=25$ and MSL with $K=55$), cross-channel dependencies and non-linear interactions cannot be adequately represented by rigid functional forms in raw signal space. Finally, edge IoT and satellite systems undergo discrete operational mode transitions (e.g., orbital day/night thermal cycling, thruster firings, Parkinsonian gait freezing, cloud server garbage collection) that cause continuous distribution drift.

\subsection{Limitations of Deep Anomaly Detectors}
Deep learning architectures provide data-driven alternatives to explicit equation modeling, yet they face three primary limitations. First, generative models (e.g., autoencoders, VAEs, masked Transformers) minimize point-level $L_1/L_2$ errors in raw signal space \cite{Hundman2018b, xu2022anomaly, wu2023timesnet}. Because reconstruction loss penalizes every discrepancy equally, autoencoders allocate capacity to fitting high-frequency sensor jitter rather than learning nominal dynamical transitions. Second, contrastive frameworks such as Neural Contextual Anomaly Detection (NCAD) \cite{carmona2021neural} train encoders via Contextual Outlier Exposure (COE) using heuristic synthetic perturbations (e.g., Gaussian noise spikes, sequence splicing). However, real physical faults manifest as subtle deviations from nominal dynamics; synthetic injections distort embedding geometry, inducing artificial decision boundaries that generate false alarms on continuous telemetry. Third, in high-dimensional observation space, even after per-channel standardization, Euclidean reconstruction errors ($\|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 = \sum_k (x_k - \hat{x}_k)^2$) accumulate stochastic residual variance across dozens of irrelevant or noisy sensors. Under automated streaming thresholding such as Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT), this residual accumulation yields heavy, unstable tail distributions that distort extreme-value calibrators, triggering false alarm flooding in operational telemetry.

\subsection{Investigating Latent Prediction and Inductive Priors}
To investigate whether operating in latent representation space mitigates observation-space noise drowning without relying on synthetic contrastive perturbations, we study Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) for multivariate telemetry. Rather than reconstructing raw, jitter-corrupted sensor observations $\hat{x} \approx x$, a JEPA maps past observations $x_{\text{ctx}}$ into an abstract representation space and predicts target representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ directly \cite{lecun2022path, assran2023self}. By predicting in latent space, the architecture discards high-frequency stochastic sensor noise that lacks temporal predictability, potentially conditioning prediction residuals for automated tail calibration.

Alongside an unconstrained latent predictor (\texttt{TS-JEPA}), we formalize and test three candidate physics-inspired regularizers hypothesized to stabilize representation dynamics. The first formulation, \textit{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, regularizes the learned latent Koopman transition matrix via spectral entropy to promote high-rank modal expressivity. The second, \textit{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, adapts hydrodynamic Reynolds decomposition ($z = \bar{z} + z'$) to penalize discrepancies in cross-coordinate second-moment fluctuation covariance. The third, \textit{Potential-Flow JEPA}, parameterizes latent transitions as gradients of a scalar potential ($\hat{v} = -\nabla \Phi$), enforcing curl-free dynamics. These architectures share a latent coordinate-saliency scoring gate and a Littlewood-Paley dyadic frequency front-end, calibrated via Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT); a strictly causal input-channel gating variant is additionally evaluated in a controlled fault-type sensitivity study.

\subsection{Contributions}
This work makes four principal contributions:

\noindent\textbf{1) Quantifying the Latent Residual Conditioning Effect:} We demonstrate that forecasting telemetry states in abstract representation space rather than reconstructing raw observation waveforms produces residual distributions with lower tail heaviness, lowering measured nominal false-positive rates under held-out SPOT from $30.0\%\text{--}30.7\%$ to $7.6\%$ (paired difference $-0.150$ against TimesNet, $p=0.007$; $-0.150$ against TranAD, $p=0.004$). However, Point-F1 and PR-AUC remain statistically indistinguishable from reconstructive baselines, while detection latency increases relative to the fastest baseline ($\Delta t = 80$ vs. $48$ steps) and strictly causal streaming incurs an event recall penalty ($10.7\%$ vs. $32.9\%$), demonstrating that latent prediction provides a conservative, low-false-alarm operating point rather than universal dominance.

\noindent\textbf{2) Rigorous Evaluation of Physics-Inspired Inductive Priors:} We evaluate three candidate physics-inspired regularizers (\texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, \texttt{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, and \texttt{Potential-Flow JEPA}) against an unconstrained latent predictor (\texttt{TS-JEPA}). Across 33 streams, neither Operator-Entropy (Point-F1 paired difference $+0.002$, $p=0.69$) nor Reynolds-Stress ($-0.015$, $p=0.50$) outperforms TS-JEPA, while Potential-Flow performs significantly worse ($-0.084$, $p=0.013$). We show that linear Koopman operators excel specifically on quasi-linear physical dynamics (spacecraft telemetry, PR-AUC $0.591$ on SMAP), whereas curl-free potential flows fail on cyclic kinematics due to dynamical misspecification. Controlled ablations on fixed neural backbones confirm that switching off the physical loss terms yields no statistically significant difference ($p \ge 0.375$).

\noindent\textbf{3) Empirical Analysis of Threshold Calibration Failures:} We document that automated EVT/SPOT calibration systematically fails to achieve its theoretical design risk ($q=10^{-3}$) on unlabelled non-stationary telemetry, resulting in empirical nominal false-positive rates of $5\text{--}8\%$ for JEPAs and $24\text{--}30\%$ for baselines due to distribution drift. We further demonstrate that heuristic kurtosis adjustments degrade rather than improve out-of-sample risk control, performing comparably to simple non-parametric quantile baselines.

\noindent\textbf{4) Open-Source, Reproducible Multi-Seed Benchmark:} We establish a fully reproducible benchmark across 33 matched telemetry streams from seven physical datasets evaluated under a strict held-out calibration protocol (chronological 65/35 train/held-out split with an unobserved isolation gap, up to five seeds, paired bootstrap confidence intervals, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests). All code, checkpoints, and evaluation logs are released open-source.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work in reconstructive, contrastive, and joint-embedding anomaly detection. Section~\ref{sec:dataset} details the multi-sensor telemetry benchmark suite and the held-out partition protocol. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_method} formalizes the JEPA formulations, latent saliency gating, dyadic wavelets, and EVT calibration. Section~\ref{sec:exp} establishes the experimental setup and evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} evaluates architectural components and inductive regularizer controls. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents quantitative benchmark results, threshold calibration analyses, and computational profiles. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Unsupervised time-series anomaly detection spans dynamical equation discovery, reconstructive generative modeling, contrastive representation learning, and predictive representation architectures. This section reviews these approaches and positions our empirical investigation into latent predictive residuals and automated threshold calibration.

\subsection{Dynamical Systems Modeling and Koopman Operators}
The physical modeling of multi-sensor telemetry has long sought to capture system behavior as continuous dynamical trajectories governed by differential equations $\dot{x}(t) = \mathbf{f}(x(t))$ \cite{brunton2016discovering}. One prominent approach, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) \cite{chen2018neural}, parameterizes continuous velocity fields using neural networks $\frac{dx}{dt} = f_\theta(x(t), t)$ and integrates trajectories numerically. While Neural ODEs learn flexible vector fields, fitting them directly in raw observation space forces numerical solvers to integrate through high-frequency stochastic sensor noise, leading to numerical stiffness, slow training, and sensitivity to measurement jitter.

In parallel, Koopman operator theory and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) \cite{brunton2016koopman, lusch2018deep, liu2023koopa} analyze non-linear dynamical systems by learning linear transition operators that act on observable functions. Modern deep Koopman frameworks (e.g., Koopa \cite{liu2023koopa}) learn observable embeddings for non-stationary forecasting. In anomaly detection, physical disruptions manifest as spectral decoherence or sudden changes in modal dissipation. Rather than operating on uncalibrated raw signals, our framework parameterizes latent linear transitions regularized by Koopman spectral entropy during training directly in representation space.

\subsection{Reconstruction-Based Detection}
The predominant deep learning approach in time-series anomaly detection relies on learning nominal patterns through raw signal reconstruction. Hundman et al. \cite{Hundman2018b} introduced LSTM-based prediction coupled with non-parametric dynamic thresholding for NASA spacecraft telemetry (SMAP/MSL). Modern architectures incorporate specialized attention and frequency-domain transformations. For example, Anomaly Transformer \cite{xu2022anomaly} introduces Anomaly-Attention to quantify the association discrepancy between local prior Gaussian kernel associations and global series self-attention. TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet} decomposes 1D multivariate time series into 2D temporal variations using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) periodicity discovery and 2D Inception blocks. Similarly, TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad} employs a two-phase adversarial Transformer with sub-series attention and focus scoring to amplify subtle reconstruction errors.

Despite their empirical popularity, reconstructive methods share a fundamental vulnerability: signal-level noise memorization. Because loss functions optimize point-level $L_1$ or $L_2$ differences in raw signal space, reconstructive networks are biased toward fitting high-frequency stochastic noise rather than learning invariant dynamical laws. In addition, during sustained anomalies, corrupted observations contaminate the context window, causing the reconstructor to adapt and reconstruct the anomaly as the nominal state.

\subsection{Forecasting-Based Detection and Threshold Calibration}
An alternative to reconstruction is temporal forecasting, which models the conditional distribution of future observations given past context. Classical autoregressive models and deep probabilistic forecasters (such as DeepAR \cite{salinas2020deepar}) predict future values and flag observations whose prediction error exceeds normal bounds. While forecasting prevents full-sequence autoencoders from memorizing anomalous segments through bidirectional receptive fields, forecasting in raw observation space remains vulnerable to the same noise accumulation problem: in high-dimensional sensor arrays, point-level Euclidean forecasting errors accumulate variance across dozens of irrelevant channels.

Following score generation, unsupervised anomaly detectors require an automated calibration rule to convert continuous residual scores into binary operational alarms without access to test labels. Hundman et al. \cite{Hundman2018b} introduced non-parametric dynamic thresholding via sliding window error distributions. More recently, extreme value theory, notably Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}, has become standard for automated thresholding, fitting Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) tails to residual exceedances under a specified risk parameter $q$. However, asymptotic EVT guarantees rely on independent and identically distributed tail observations. On real-world multi-sensor telemetry, temporal autocorrelation, non-stationary operational drift, and heavy tails can cause extreme-value calibrators to deviate substantially from their nominal design risk.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning and Representation-Based Detection}
To avoid reconstructing raw noise, contrastive methods learn discriminative representations by separating nominal sequences from anomalous perturbations. Neural Contextual Anomaly Detection (NCAD) \cite{carmona2021neural} uses Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) trained with Contextual Outlier Exposure (COE), injecting artificial synthetic anomalies (Gaussian noise, point spikes, trend shifts) into training windows to create negative pairs. However, synthetic injections distort the physical dynamical manifold and fail to generalize to subtle real-world physical faults (such as gradual thermal dissipation or Parkinsonian gait freezing). Alternatively, DCdetector \cite{yang2023dcdetector} replaces reconstruction with multi-scale dual-attention contrastive representation learning, maximizing cross-scale representation consistency. While DCdetector eliminates artificial injection heuristics, contrastive objectives require careful scale selection and representation pooling.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Time-Series Representation Learning and JEPAs}
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA), originally proposed by LeCun \cite{lecun2022path} and demonstrated in computer vision by I-JEPA \cite{assran2023self} and V-JEPA \cite{bardes2024vjepa}, predict target representations from context in latent space. Non-contrastive regularizers such as Barlow Twins \cite{zbontar2021barlow}, W-MSE \cite{ermolov2021whitening}, and VICReg \cite{bardes2021vicreg} prevent representation collapse without negative mining.

Recent works have extended self-supervised and predictive representation learning principles to time-series sequences. TS2Vec \cite{yue2022ts2vec} learns multi-scale representations through contextual and temporal contrasting, while Eldele et al. \cite{eldele2021time} enforce temporal-contextual cross-view consistency. Systematic surveys by Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2023self} document the emergence of self-supervised representation architectures across multi-sensor streams. However, existing time-series representation frameworks typically evaluate continuous ranking metrics (PR-AUC) or post-hoc threshold sweeps, without systematically studying how latent prediction residuals behave under automated statistical threshold calibration.

\subsection{Physics-Inspired Deep Learning and Structured Inductive Biases}
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) \cite{raissi2019physics}, alongside Hamiltonian \cite{greydanus2019hamiltonian} and Lagrangian Neural Networks \cite{cranmer2020lagrangian}, integrate dynamical principles into neural architectures. However, while Hamiltonian networks enforce strict energy conservation for closed mechanical systems, standard PINNs face two primary limitations in high-dimensional sensor telemetry. First, jointly optimizing data fidelity and PDE residual loss leads to stiff, competing gradient dynamics, often causing training instability or failure to converge in high noise regimes \cite{wang2021understanding}. Second, complex IoT and aerospace telemetry (e.g., SMAP, MSL, and cyber-physical water networks) rarely follow a single closed-form PDE whose analytical derivatives can be evaluated across dozens of uncalibrated sensors. Rather than enforcing analytical differential equations through stiff boundary constraints, we examine whether incorporating structured dynamical inductive biases (such as spectral entropy, second-moment covariance matching, or conservative potential flows) into latent representation spaces can improve predictive stability and condition residuals for automated tail calibration.

\subsection{Clarification of Technical Terminology}
To prevent ambiguity, we establish precise definitions for recurring technical terms used throughout this manuscript:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physics-Inspired vs. Physics-Informed:} Physics-informed methods enforce known governing equations (such as Navier-Stokes or wave equations) directly via loss residuals. In contrast, physics-inspired methods adopt mathematical structures suggested by physical theories (such as spectral entropy or second-moment closures) as exploratory inductive regularizers on learned representations, without claiming that the latent coordinates correspond to physical state variables.
    \item \textbf{Causal Preprocessing vs. Causal Decision-Time Access:} Causal preprocessing indicates that feature transformations (such as the input-channel gating variant) are computed strictly from historical context observations ($\partial \mathbf{g}/\partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$). Causal decision-time access specifies whether operational alarms are issued with zero lookahead latency ($0$-lookahead) or after collecting a buffered prospective window ($S$-lookahead).
    \item \textbf{Conservative vs. Dissipative:} Conservative dynamics preserve total energy and have zero curl ($\nabla \times v = 0$). Autonomous gradient flows $\dot{z} = -\nabla\Phi$ are strictly dissipative with respect to the potential $\Phi$, satisfying $\frac{d}{d\tau}\Phi \le 0$ and precluding periodic orbits.
    \item \textbf{Operator Non-Degeneracy vs. Representation Anti-Collapse:} Operator non-degeneracy ensures that the learned transition operator $\mathbf{K}$ does not collapse to zero or rank 1. Representation anti-collapse refers to the separate property that the encoder does not map distinct time-series inputs to a constant latent vector.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positioning of the Proposed Framework}
This investigation bridges joint-embedding predictive learning and multivariate time-series anomaly detection through four structural elements. First, by forecasting future representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ rather than reconstructing raw observations $\hat{x}$, our models avoid high-frequency noise drowning that affects reconstructive baselines (TimesNet, TranAD). Second, we formulate three candidate physics-inspired regularizers: Operator-Entropy (spectral rank preservation), Reynolds-Stress (second-moment fluctuation alignment), and Potential-Flow (curl-free gradient dynamics), evaluating them empirically against unconstrained latent controls. Third, we apply a latent coordinate-saliency gate that amplifies sparse predictive residuals in representation space, evaluate a strictly causal input-channel gating variant for isolating active sensors without target leakage, and use Littlewood-Paley dyadic frequency shells to separate transient impulses from low-frequency operational drift. Finally, we evaluate Extreme Value Theory (EVT / SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly} on strictly held-out nominal telemetry splits to quantify the gap between theoretical design risk and empirical false-alarm rates.

\section{Multi-Sensor IoT and Remote Sensing Benchmark Suite}
\label{sec:dataset}

To evaluate Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures and candidate physical regularizers across diverse edge, cyber-physical, and orbital sensing dynamics, our benchmark suite comprises 11 datasets spanning 45 continuous telemetry streams sourced from NASA spacecraft archives \cite{Hundman2018b}, the UCI Machine Learning Repository \cite{bachlin2010wearable, asuncion2007uci}, TimeEval \cite{schmidl2022anomaly}, and mTSBench \cite{zhou2026mtsbench}. We evaluate 33 streams under our primary multi-seed held-out calibration protocol and reserve 12 streams for extended operational case studies. We organize these streams into four physical sensing domains:

\subsection{Domain 1: Satellite and Spacecraft Telemetry (13 Streams)}
This domain covers high-reliability orbital and deep-space telemetry. The \textit{NASA SMAP Earth Observation Satellite} \cite{Hundman2018b} (6 evaluated streams: \texttt{A-1}, \texttt{A-2}, \texttt{A-7}, \texttt{D-3}, \texttt{P-3}, \texttt{E-1}) provides continuous telemetry values alongside one-hot command vectors monitoring power, thermal, and attitude control subsystems. The \textit{NASA MSL Curiosity Rover} \cite{Hundman2018b} (6 standardized streams: \texttt{C-1}, \texttt{C-2}, \texttt{D-14}, \texttt{D-15}, \texttt{D-16}, \texttt{M-1}; 4 retained in the held-out benchmark and 2 reserved for case studies due to short nominal blocks) tracks robotic actuator, joint drive, and environmental streams during planetary surface operations. Finally, the \textit{SWAN-SF Space Weather Telemetry} \cite{angryk2020multivariate} (1 stream) provides solar photospheric vector magnetogram telemetry from NASA SDO/HMI, tracking active region magnetic field perturbations during solar eruptive events.

\subsection{Domain 2: Wearable Biometrics and Kinematics (6 Streams)}
This domain evaluates human kinematic telemetry via the \textit{Daphnet Wearable Body Sensor Network} \cite{bachlin2010wearable} (6 evaluated streams: \texttt{S01R01E1} to \texttt{S03R02E0}). Telemetry consists of 64~Hz tri-axial accelerometry recorded from wearable sensor nodes placed on the shank, thigh, and trunk to track Freezing-of-Gait (FoG) motor breakdown events in Parkinson's disease patients. At 64~Hz, the suspect horizon $S = 64$ corresponds to a 1.0-second lookahead window.

\subsection{Domain 3: Industrial Processes and Cyber-Physical Systems (16 Streams)}
This domain covers continuous industrial processes and fluid transportation. The \textit{GHL Gasoil Heating Loop} (14 benchmark streams) captures thermal and fluid process loop telemetry across temperature, flow rate, and valve pressure dynamics. The \textit{Genesis Automation Testbed} (1 stream) tracks manufacturing automation telemetry from an industrial pick-and-place demonstrator during mechanical handling faults. The \textit{GECCO Water Distribution IoT} (1 stream) monitors physical water distribution infrastructure through continuous chemical and hydrological measurements at 1-minute resolution (where $S=64$ represents a 64-minute operational horizon).

\subsection{Domain 4: Smart Infrastructure and Environmental Sensing (10 Streams)}
This domain evaluates urban infrastructure and cyber-physical networks. The \textit{Room-Occupancy Environmental IoT} dataset \cite{candanedo2016accurate} (2 streams) records ambient environmental conditions ($\text{CO}_2$, light, temperature, humidity) tracking room-occupancy state switches. The \textit{CICIDS Edge Network Telemetry} (6 streams) captures network flow dynamics, tracking gateway protocol shifts and security anomalies including DDoS bursts and port scans. The \textit{CalIt2 Building People-Flow Telemetry} \cite{asuncion2007uci} (1 stream) records continuous pedestrian counts via optical beam sensors at the UC Irvine Calit2 facility entrance. Finally, the \textit{Metro Highway Traffic} dataset (1 stream) tracks interstate highway traffic volume on Minnesota Interstate I-94, where anomalies correspond to major traffic disruption and incident events.

Each benchmark stream provides nominal training sequences and testing sequences containing labeled ground-truth anomalies. Continuous telemetry channels are standardized via training statistics ($\bm{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \bm{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$), flooring standard deviations at $\max(\bm{\sigma}, 10^{-2})$ to prevent division by zero during flatline operational phases. While mission archives contain larger entity counts (the complete SMAP archive includes 55 telemetry entities and MSL includes 27 entities across subsystems), our benchmark evaluates standardized subsets studied in the telemetry anomaly detection literature \cite{Hundman2018b, schmidl2022anomaly} to ensure strict matching and reproducible runtime conditions across all baselines.

To eliminate circular in-sample calibration and measure out-of-sample false-alarm rates under automated thresholding, each stream's nominal training sequence is partitioned chronologically into training (65\%), calibration (17.5\%), and held-out evaluation (17.5\%) blocks separated by an isolation gap (detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:exp_setup}). This held-out calibration protocol spans 33 streams across seven physical datasets: 6 Daphnet (wearable kinematics), 14 GHL (fluid transport), 4 MSL (planetary rovers), 6 SMAP (Earth observation satellites), Genesis (manufacturing automation), Metro (highway traffic), and SWAN (solar space weather). Streams with nominal blocks too short to support disjoint calibration and evaluation blocks (CalIt2, Room-Occupancy, and two MSL streams) or where multi-seed stride-1 bootstrap evaluation over $10^6$ steps was computationally prohibitive (GECCO, CICIDS) are reserved for operational case studies, as documented in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_specs}.

Table~\ref{tab:dataset_specs} provides the technical specifications across the benchmark suite, totaling $2{,}079{,}507$ nominal training timesteps and $2{,}986{,}574$ test timesteps (over 5.06 million observations). We explicitly document the physical domain, channel dimension $K$, sampling resolution, sequence lengths, anomaly contamination ratio $\pi\%$, anomaly failure modes, and partition protocols to ensure experimental reproducibility.

\label{sec:proposed_method}

This section formalizes the mathematical formulation, latent predictors, and inductive biases of the proposed framework. 

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Latent Space Prediction}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$ denote a continuous multivariate time-series sequence consisting of $K$ observed sensor variables over $T$ timesteps. The sequence is partitioned into historical context $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ and suspect/target horizon $x_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$, where $C$ and $S$ denote the respective temporal horizons.

Existing deep learning baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer) frame anomaly detection as pointwise reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
    \min_\theta \mathbb{E}\left[ \| \mathbf{X} - \mathcal{D}_\theta(\mathcal{E}_\theta(\mathbf{X})) \|_F^2 \right].
\end{equation}
In complex multi-sensor telemetry, ambient high-frequency stochastic jitter dominates the Frobenius norm, causing models to waste representational bandwidth reconstructing sensor noise rather than modeling system state dynamics (leading to noise drowning).

To overcome this, we adopt the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) framework \cite{lecun2022path}. A context encoder $E_\theta$ maps $x_{\text{ctx}} \to z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, while an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) momentum target encoder $E_\phi$ maps $x_{\text{tgt}} \to z_{\text{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, updated via:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k \leftarrow m_k \phi_{k-1} + (1 - m_k) \theta_k, \quad m_k \in [0.996, 0.9995].
\end{equation}
The core objective is to forecast target representations directly in latent space, testing whether structured inductive priors condition prediction residuals for automated tail calibration.

\subsection{Latent Predictor Architectures and Dynamical Inductive Priors}
\label{subsec:triad_formulations}
To examine whether incorporating structured dynamical inductive biases stabilizes representation forecasting, we evaluate an unconstrained latent predictor alongside three candidate formulations inspired by physical dynamical principles:

\subsubsection{Operator-Entropy JEPA (Spectral Dynamics \& Modal Dispersion)}
In dynamical systems theory, non-linear trajectories can be analyzed via linear operators acting on observable functions (Koopman operator theory \cite{koopman1931hamiltonian, mezic2005spectral}). In our architecture, the encoder maps the observation window to a finite-dimensional latent observable vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^D$. We parameterize the predictive transition between context and target latent states via a learned transition operator $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}.
\end{equation}
To prevent the operator from collapsing onto a degenerate low-rank subspace (dimensional collapse) or degenerating to zero (energy collapse), we formulate an operator-entropy regularizer that decouples spectral uniformity from total operator energy.

Let $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_D \ge 0$ denote the singular values of $\mathbf{K}$, with corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2$ of the symmetric Gram matrix $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T$. The total operator Frobenius energy is $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}) = \|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^D \lambda_i$. For any non-zero operator ($\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}) > 0$), we define the scale-free spectral density distribution $\mathbf{p} = [p_1, \dots, p_D]^T$ over latent modes:
\begin{equation}
    p_i = \frac{\lambda_i(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T)}{\sum_{j=1}^D \lambda_j(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T)} = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2}, \quad \sum_{i=1}^D p_i = 1, \quad p_i \ge 0.
\end{equation}
We omit isotropic diagonal loading ($\frac{\epsilon}{D}\mathbf{I}$) from the Gram matrix before normalization; adding it would cause $\mathbf{K} \to \mathbf{0}$ to yield an artificial identity spectrum with maximal entropy, spuriously rewarding total collapse. Instead, we evaluate the Von Neumann spectral entropy directly on the scale-free modal probabilities with the standard analytic convention $0 \log 0 = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = -\sum_{i: p_i > 0} p_i \log p_i.
\end{equation}
The \textit{Spectral Entropy Deficiency} quantifies the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the uniform modal equipartition $\mathbf{u} = [1/D, \dots, 1/D]^T$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K}) = \log D - \mathcal{H}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\mathbf{p} \,\|\, \mathbf{u}) \ge 0.
\end{equation}
Here, $\Delta\mathcal{H} = 0$ if and only if all modes carry equal energy ($\sigma_1 = \dots = \sigma_D$), while dimensional collapse to rank 1 yields maximal deficiency $\Delta\mathcal{H} = \log D$.

To penalize energy collapse ($\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0$) and prevent explosive gain, we introduce a non-negative Bregman energy barrier relative to nominal isometric scale $\mathcal{E}_0 = \gamma D$ ($\gamma = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{K}) = r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) - 1 - \log r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) \ge 0, \quad r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) = \max\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2}{\gamma D}, \epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ provides a numerical floor. For the identity operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{I}$ with $\gamma=1.0$, $\|\mathbf{I}\|_F^2 = D$, yielding $r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{I}) = 1.0 > \epsilon$ and $\Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{I}) = 1 - 1 - \log 1 = 0$ exactly. We emphasize that the implemented barrier is \emph{floored}: it attains a finite maximum penalty $\epsilon - 1 - \log \epsilon \approx 12.8$ (before the $\beta_{\text{norm}}$ weighting) throughout the region $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2 < \epsilon \gamma D$, rather than diverging. The divergence to $+\infty$ as $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0$ is a property only of the unfloored idealization $\Phi_{\text{energy}}^{\ast}(\mathbf{K}) = r - 1 - \log r$ with $r = \|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2 / (\gamma D)$. In our architecture, the transition operator $\mathbf{K}$ is initialized near identity ($\mathbf{K} \sim \mathbf{I} + \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, 0.01^2 \mathbf{I})$), ensuring $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2 \approx D$ and $r_{\text{safe}} \approx 1.0$, which starts training with a vanishing energy penalty ($\Phi_{\text{energy}} \approx 0$). Because latent vectors $z$ are normalized via LayerNorm ($\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_2^2] \approx D$), the isometric reference scale $\mathcal{E}_0 = \gamma D$ with $\gamma = 1.0$ matches the natural energy scale of unitary transitions, preserving variance without artificial scaling. In sensitivity checks across $\lambda_{\text{ent}} \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$, the model converged stably without numerical stiffness, with $\lambda_{\text{ent}} = 0.5$ providing a balanced trade-off between spectral spread and predictive regression. The composite spectral regularizer is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = \Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K}) + \beta_{\text{norm}} \Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{K}),
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{\text{norm}} = 1.0$. The total training objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Op-Entropy}} = \| \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}} - z_{\text{tgt}} \|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{ent}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{ent}} = 0.5$ (the value used in all experiments). All shared representation components (latent coordinate saliency gating, dyadic frequency shells, and LayerNorm) are held strictly fixed across models and controls, ensuring that performance differences isolate the transition operator loss and parameterization. We emphasize that $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ acts directly on the transition operator matrix $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$, enforcing a non-collapsed, full-rank operator spectrum; it does not provide an analytical guarantee against representation collapse in the encoder itself. In practice, representation collapse in the encoder is mitigated through the asymmetric Exponential Moving Average (EMA) target encoder dynamics and stop-gradient operations common to self-supervised frameworks (e.g., BYOL \cite{grill2020bootstrap}, I-JEPA \cite{assran2023self}), rather than by $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ directly.

\begin{proposition}[Operator Triviality Invariance]
\label{prop:anti_collapse}
Let $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \|\mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)} - z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)}\|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{ent}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ with $\lambda_{\text{ent}} > 0, \beta_{\text{norm}} > 0$, non-trivial target batch $\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \|z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)}\|_2^2 = E_{\text{tgt}} > 0$, and non-degenerate context--target coupling $\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)} {z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)}}^T \neq \mathbf{0}$. Then (i) the zero operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ is strictly not a local or global minimizer of $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K})$ under the implemented floored barrier, and (ii) under the unfloored idealization ($\epsilon \to 0^+$, $\Phi_{\text{energy}}^{\ast} = r - 1 - \log r$), additionally $\lim_{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) = +\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) At $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ the implemented barrier is saturated at its floor: $r_{\text{safe}} = \epsilon$ is constant on a neighborhood of $\mathbf{0}$, so $\Phi_{\text{energy}}$ contributes zero gradient there, while the prediction term has gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}\big|_{\mathbf{0}} = -\frac{2}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)} {z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)}}^T \neq \mathbf{0}$ by the coupling assumption. Hence $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ is not a stationary point and cannot be a local minimizer; the floored penalty remains finite, $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{0}) \le \log D + \beta_{\text{norm}}(\epsilon - 1 - \log \epsilon) \approx \log D + 12.8\,\beta_{\text{norm}}$. (ii) For the unfloored barrier, $-\log r \to +\infty$ as $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0^+$ while $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} \to E_{\text{tgt}} < \infty$, so $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) \to +\infty$; the identity operator satisfies $\Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{I}) = \Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{I}) = 0$ with strictly finite loss, so $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ cannot be a global minimizer in the idealization either. The divergence statement (ii) applies to the unfloored limit only; the anti-triviality guarantee for the trained objective rests on (i).
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Remark (Operator Non-Degeneracy vs. Representation Collapse):} Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse} establishes that the transition operator $\mathbf{K}$ cannot degenerate to $\mathbf{0}$ or collapse to rank 1 when operating on non-trivial target representations. However, it does not prevent encoder representation collapse. If the encoder $E_\theta$ maps all inputs to a constant representation $z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)} = z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)} = \mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, the operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{I}$ achieves zero prediction error and zero spectral penalty while carrying zero information about the input time series. Prevention of constant representation collapse depends empirically on the stop-gradient momentum encoder dynamics rather than Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse}.

\subsubsection{Reynolds-Stress JEPA (Second-Moment Closure Head)}
\label{subsubsec:reynolds_stress_jepa}
Reynolds decomposition splits a turbulent velocity field into a mean flow and a fluctuation component, where the Reynolds stress tensor captures the second moment of turbulent velocity fluctuations. We adapt the second-moment formulation as an inductive bias in latent representation space. The latent coordinates are learned feature embeddings rather than physical velocity components, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is not a physical momentum-transport tensor.

The context encoder outputs latent representation $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, from which a two-layer MLP predicts the conditional mean target latent $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}$. The momentum target encoder (EMA, stop-gradient) produces the target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$. A learned linear projection $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s \times D}$ ($d_s = 16$) yields the empirical second-moment matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}} = (\mathbf{P} z_{\text{tgt}})(\mathbf{P} z_{\text{tgt}})^T + 10^{-3}\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{S}_{++}^{d_s \times d_s}.
\end{equation}
Because $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}}$ is computed from an instantaneous latent vector rather than averaged over time across the target horizon $S$, it forms a rank-one outer product regularized by a ridge term to enforce membership in the open cone of symmetric positive-definite matrices $\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d_s \times d_s}$. Statistically, it imposes an auxiliary second-moment projection penalty. To ensure that predicted tensors respect this geometry, the closure head $h_\psi(z_{\text{ctx}})$ outputs the elements of a lower-triangular matrix $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s \times d_s}$ with positive diagonal elements parameterized as $L_{ii} = \operatorname{softplus}(a_{ii}) + \epsilon$. The predicted covariance is reconstructed via Cholesky factorization:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T + 10^{-3}\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{S}_{++}^{d_s \times d_s},
\end{equation}
which strictly confines predictions to $\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d_s \times d_s}$ for all context inputs. The training objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Reynolds}} = \operatorname{MSE}(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}, z_{\text{tgt}}) + 0.1\, \operatorname{MSE}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}}).
\end{equation}
At inference time, the anomaly score combines the mean latent discrepancy and the fluctuation tensor residual via the coordinate-saliency gate (formalized in Section~\ref{subsec:scoring_function}). The stress term enters both the training loss and the test score; its downstream contribution is evaluated in Section~\ref{subsec:eval_physics_priors} and Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a).

\subsubsection{Potential-Flow JEPA (Conservative Field Theory and Flow-Matching Derivation)}
\label{subsubsec:potential_flow_jepa}
Under the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition theorem, any sufficiently smooth vector field $v \in C^1(\mathbb{R}^D, \mathbb{R}^D)$ can be resolved into an irrotational (curl-free) conservative potential gradient and a solenoidal (divergence-free) rotational field:
\begin{equation}
    v = -\nabla \Phi + \nabla \times A.
\end{equation}
In nominal physical equilibria, dissipative dynamical systems relax toward local minima on potential energy surfaces. Continuous latent trajectory evolution can be modeled as a dissipative gradient flow:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{z}(\tau) = -\nabla_z \Phi_\psi(z(\tau)),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_\psi: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar potential network parameterized with smooth $C^2$ activation functions (e.g., SiLU).

\begin{proposition}[Absence of Periodic Attractors in Gradient Flows]
\label{prop:no_limit_cycles}
Let $\dot{z} = -\nabla\Phi(z)$ be an autonomous gradient flow on $\mathbb{R}^D$ where $\Phi \in C^2(\mathbb{R}^D, \mathbb{R})$. There exist no non-constant periodic orbits.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\gamma(t)$ is a periodic orbit with period $T_p > 0$ such that $\gamma(t + T_p) = \gamma(t)$. Integrating the time derivative of $\Phi$ along the closed path yields:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{T_p} \frac{d\Phi(\gamma(t))}{dt} dt = \Phi(\gamma(T_p)) - \Phi(\gamma(0)) = 0.
\end{equation}
Applying the chain rule, $\frac{d\Phi(\gamma(t))}{dt} = \langle \nabla\Phi(\gamma(t)), \dot{\gamma}(t) \rangle = -\|\nabla\Phi(\gamma(t))\|_2^2 \le 0$. Since the integrand is non-positive, its integral can equal zero if and only if $\|\nabla\Phi(\gamma(t))\|_2 \equiv 0$ for all $t \in [0, T_p]$. Hence $\dot{\gamma}(t) \equiv \mathbf{0}$, meaning $\gamma(t)$ is an isolated fixed point, contradicting the assumption of a non-constant periodic orbit.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Continuous Velocity Field and Multi-Subspace Regime Routing}
Rather than parameterizing a discrete unconstrained displacement, the continuous velocity field is constructed as the negative gradient of a parameterized scalar potential field $\Phi_\psi$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{v}(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) = -\nabla_{z_t} \Phi_\psi(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_\psi$ is a three-layer residual MLP with LayerNorm and SiLU activations. The network is conditioned on the current state $z_t$, the continuous diffusion time $t \in [0, 1]$ via sinusoidal embeddings, the context representation $z_{\text{ctx}}$, and a discrete operational regime projection $p_{\text{regime}}$. The raw Euclidean gradient is projected onto the tangent space orthogonal to unit vector $u = z_t / \|z_t\|_2$ via $v_\perp = v - \langle u, v \rangle u$, stabilizing discrete trajectory integration and bounding curvature.

Discrete operational modes are captured via $K_r = 4$ learnable orthonormal subspace projection matrices $\mathbf{U}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d}$ ($d=8, D=32$, satisfying $\mathbf{U}_k^\top \mathbf{U}_k = \mathbf{I}_d$). The active regime projection $p_{\text{regime}}$ is selected by identifying the subspace that captures maximal context energy:
\begin{equation}
    k^* = \arg\max_{k \in \{1,\dots,K_r\}} \|\mathbf{U}_k^\top z_{\text{ctx}}\|_2^2, \quad p_{\text{regime}} = \mathbf{U}_{k^*} \mathbf{U}_{k^*}^\top z_{\text{ctx}}.
\end{equation}
During training, the subspace frames are regularized with an orthogonality separation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{subspace}} = \frac{2}{K_r(K_r-1)} \sum_{i < j} \|\mathbf{U}_i^\top \mathbf{U}_j\|_F^2$. In Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations}, a single-regime control ($K_r=1$, $p_{\text{regime}} = \mathbf{0}$) evaluates performance when regime routing is disabled.

\paragraph{Flow-Matching Objective, Numerical Midpoint Evaluation, and Dynamical Misspecification}
We distinguish the continuous dynamical system from the empirical learning and inference objective. The theoretical Lyapunov dissipation in Proposition~\ref{prop:no_limit_cycles} governs autonomous continuous gradient descent $\dot{z} = -\nabla\Phi(z)$. In contrast, our architecture trains the parameterized velocity field $\hat{v}(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) = -\nabla_{z_t}\Phi_\psi(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}})$ via conditional flow matching (OT-CFM) along linear interpolation paths between Gaussian noise $z_{\text{noise}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and target latent representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = (1-t)z_{\text{noise}} + t z_{\text{tgt}}, \quad t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
The target conditional vector field along this optimal transport displacement interpolant is:
\begin{equation}
    v_t^*(z_t \mid z_{\text{tgt}}, z_{\text{noise}}) = \frac{d}{dt} z_t = z_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{noise}}.
\end{equation}
The conditional flow-matching training loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Pot-Flow}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, z_{\text{noise}}} \left[ \| \hat{v}(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) - (z_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{noise}}) \|_2^2 \right].
\end{equation}
Along the deterministic mean trajectory ($z_{\text{noise}} = \mathbf{0}$), the interpolation simplifies to $z_t = t z_{\text{tgt}}$ and the reference target velocity is constant: $v_t^* = z_{\text{tgt}}$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$.

At inference time, rather than numerically integrating an ODE from $t=0$ to $t=1$ via multi-step numerical solvers, the detector queries the velocity field at the trajectory midpoint $t=0.5$ with state $z_{\text{eval}} = 0.5 z_{\text{tgt}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{v}(0.5 z_{\text{tgt}}, 0.5, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) - z_{\text{tgt}}.
\end{equation}
Because the exact reference target velocity along the mean path is $v_{0.5}^* = z_{\text{tgt}}$, this midpoint evaluation directly measures the discrepancy between the learned potential gradient and the nominal target representation. This formulation matches the dimensionality ($D=32$) and Euclidean scale ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ under LayerNorm) of the unconstrained TS-JEPA residual $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}}$. The anomaly score is computed by passing $\mathbf{e}(t)$ through the latent coordinate-saliency gate: $\mathcal{S}(t) = g(\mathbf{e}(t))$.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamical Misspecification on Non-Conservative Telemetry:} Constraining the velocity field to be curl-free ($\nabla \times \hat{v} \equiv \mathbf{0}$) guarantees that the circulation along any closed path $\mathcal{C}$ in state space vanishes identically:
\begin{equation}
    \oint_{\mathcal{C}} \hat{v} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0.
\end{equation}
While valid for purely dissipative physical equilibria, real-world multi-sensor telemetry routinely records non-conservative cyclic dynamics (such as motor revolutions, diurnal hydrological consumption cycles in GECCO, and periodic gait kinematics in Daphnet). Enforcing an irrotational velocity field creates fundamental dynamical misspecification by penalizing normal circulating orbits in representation space. This structural mismatch predicts degraded detection on cyclic telemetry, a hypothesis evaluated empirically in Sections~\ref{subsec:eval_physics_priors} and~\ref{subsec:physics_diagnostics}.

\subsection{Anomaly Scoring and Operational Detection Latency}
\label{subsec:scoring_function}
During inference, sliding context windows $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :]$ and suspect target windows $x_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :]$ are processed with a stride of 1 timestep. For TS-JEPA, Operator-Entropy JEPA, and Potential-Flow JEPA, the anomaly score is computed by applying the latent coordinate-saliency gate $g(\cdot)$ (Section~\ref{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}) to the latent discrepancy vector $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}(t) - z_{\text{tgt}}(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}(t) = g(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}(t) - z_{\text{tgt}}(t)).
\end{equation}
For Reynolds-Stress JEPA, the score incorporates both the gated mean discrepancy and the gated fluctuation tensor residual: $\mathcal{S}(t) = g(z_{\text{tgt}} - \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}) + 0.5\, g(\operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}}))$. All shared representation stages (latent coordinate saliency gating, Littlewood-Paley dyadic frequency shells, and LayerNorm) are held strictly fixed across all JEPA variants and ablation controls in Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations} to isolate the effects of the predictive regularizers.

\noindent\textbf{Operational Distinction: Zero-Lookahead Causal Filtering vs. Buffered Predictive Early Warning.}
In operational telemetry pipelines, two distinct deployment regimes exist: (1) zero-lookahead point filtering, which outputs an anomaly label $\hat{y}_t$ using only observations available up to time $t$; and (2) buffered predictive monitoring, where the architecture forecasts representations across an operational lookahead buffer $S$, raising prospective alerts for upcoming failures. TS-JEPA operates under regime (2): given historical context $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t-C:t, :]$, it projects future latent trajectory $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}$ over $[t, t+S]$. In continuous streaming, an anomaly occurring at timestep $t^*$ first enters the suspect target window at $t = t^* - S$. Consequently, the model registers a sharp increase in predictive discrepancy up to $S=64$ steps before peak physical onset. In our benchmark, we enforce strict unadjusted point-level evaluation at each decision time $t$ ($\text{stride}=1$). Because ground-truth point labels are strictly aligned to the physical anomaly window $[t^*, t^* + L]$, early warnings generated during the pre-onset interval $[t^* - S, t^*]$ are formally scored as point-level false alarms ($FP$). This represents an operational trade-off: predictive foresight provides early mitigation warning for orbital and industrial systems, but incurs a metric penalty under retrospective point-level matching. In operational incident analysis (Section VII.D), we also evaluate \textit{Middle} alignment, where the anomaly score is centered at $t + S/2$ to balance pre-onset and post-onset window overlap. Baseline models (TimesNet, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer, DCdetector, and NCAD) are evaluated under the exact identical sliding-window evaluation protocol, lookahead horizon, and standardization.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Streaming Anomaly Scoring and Decision Calibration}
\label{alg:scoring_protocol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Streaming telemetry $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$, filtration $\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{X}[1:t, :])$, context horizon $C=256$, suspect horizon $S=64$, deployment mode $\mathcal{M} \in \{\text{Causal}, \text{Buffered}\}$, calibrator $\mathcal{C}_{\text{EVT}}$ fit to nominal residuals.
\Ensure Point-level alarm stream $\hat{y}_t \in \{0, 1\}$ for $t \in [C, T]$.
\State Initialize score buffer $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^T \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$, hit counts $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^T \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$.
\For{$t = C$ \textbf{to} $T - S$}
    \State Extract historical context: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :]$ \Comment{$\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable}
    \State Encode latent context: $z_{\text{ctx}} \leftarrow E_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$
    \State Forecast future latent representation: $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow P_\psi(z_{\text{ctx}})$
    \If{$\mathcal{M} = \text{Causal}$ \textbf{($0$-Lookahead Delay)}}
        \State Target is trailing window: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}^{\text{causal}} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[t - S : t, :]$ \Comment{Strictly $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable}
        \State Encode target: $z_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow E_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}^{\text{causal}})$
        \State Compute trailing predictive discrepancy: $s_t \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{\text{sal}}(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}})$
        \State $\hat{y}_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(s_t > \tau_{\text{eff}})$ \Comment{Instantaneous decision at timestamp $t$}
    \ElsIf{$\mathcal{M} = \text{Buffered}$ \textbf{($S$-Lookahead Delay)}}
        \State Extract prospective suspect horizon: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :]$ \Comment{$\mathcal{F}_{t+S}$-measurable}
        \State Encode target representation: $z_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow E_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$
        \State Compute forward predictive discrepancy: $\mathcal{D}_t \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{\text{sal}}(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}})$
        \For{$\tau = t$ \textbf{to} $t + S - 1$}
            \State Accumulate suspect overlap: $s_\tau \leftarrow s_\tau + \mathcal{D}_t$, \quad $c_\tau \leftarrow c_\tau + 1$
        \EndFor
        \State Finalize running average score at step $t$: $\bar{s}_t \leftarrow s_t / \max(c_t, 1)$
        \State $\hat{y}_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(\bar{s}_t > \tau_{\text{eff}})$ \Comment{Finalized at timestamp $t+S$}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return Binary alarm sequence $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Shared Internal Inductive Biases}
\label{subsec:shared_inductive_biases}
To stabilize learning across high-dimensional, noisy telemetry, all three architectures incorporate three inductive mechanisms:

\subsubsection{Coordinate Saliency Gating and the Auxiliary Channel-Gate Variant}
\label{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}
On high-dimensional multivariate streams ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults frequently originate within a localized subsystem (e.g., a single thruster, thermal sensor, or battery cell), while the remaining $K-1$ channels exhibit nominal ambient noise. In isotropic representations, localized anomalies are severely diluted by the accumulated background jitter of quiescent dimensions.

The deployed detector mitigates dimensional drowning in latent space: an anti-drowning coordinate saliency gate is applied to latent error vectors $\mathbf{e} = z_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{pred}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ during discrepancy scoring:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\text{sal}}(\mathbf{e}) = (1 - \alpha) \|\mathbf{e}\|_2 + \alpha \sqrt{D \sum_{d=1}^D w_d e_d^2}, \quad w_d = \frac{\exp(|e_d| / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^D \exp(|e_j| / \tau)},
\end{equation}
which amplifies sparse latent coordinate deviations by up to $\sqrt{D}$ while maintaining exact equivalence to $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2$ when errors are isotropic ($e_1 = \dots = e_D$). This is the discrepancy gate used in every experiment: input sensor channels are encoded unweighted, and the gate acts exclusively on the $D$-dimensional predictive residual.

\paragraph{Auxiliary Input-Channel Gate (Evaluated Variant)}
To prevent dimensional drowning in input space without introducing target-window leakage, we also formulate a strictly causal \textit{Causal Channel Saliency Gate} as an auxiliary variant, evaluated separately from the deployed pipeline in the fault-type sensitivity study of Section~\ref{subsec:gate_fault_study}. Given historical context $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t-C:t, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ and prospective target horizon $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t:t+S, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$, the channel dispersion metrics are computed \textit{strictly and exclusively} from past context observations:
\begin{equation}
    v_k^{\text{ctx}} = \operatorname{Var}_{\tau \in [t-C, t]}(x_{\tau, k}) = \frac{1}{C-1} \sum_{\tau=t-C}^t \left(x_{\tau, k} - \bar{x}_k^{\text{ctx}}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_k = \frac{v_k^{\text{ctx}}}{\sum_{j=1}^K v_j^{\text{ctx}} + \epsilon}, \quad g_k = \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid, $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ prevents division by zero, $\bar{s} = 1/K$ represents the uniform variance share expectation across all $K$ channels, and $\sigma_s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (s_k - \bar{s})^2 + \epsilon}$ denotes the cross-channel dispersion of variance shares. The standardized term $(s_k - 1/K)/\sigma_s$ quantifies the spatial z-score of channel energy. 

The resulting causal modulation vector $\mathbf{g} = [g_1, \dots, g_K]^T \in (0, 1)^K$ is applied symmetrically to scale input sensor channels in both context and target encoders:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{ctx}}[:, k] = \mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}}[:, k] \cdot g_k, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{tgt}}[:, k] = \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}[:, k] \cdot g_k.
\end{equation}
Because $\mathbf{g}$ depends exclusively on $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}}$, we have:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial g_k}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}} \equiv \mathbf{0}, \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots, K\},
\end{equation}
guaranteeing exact zero-leakage shielding: future transient spikes or anomalies in the suspect target window $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}$ cannot artificially amplify channel weights prior to encoding. For univariate streams ($K = 1$), the formulation degenerates ($s_1 = \bar{s} = 1$, $\sigma_s = 0$, so $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ is not identity); the implementation therefore bypasses the sigmoid and returns $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{1}$ explicitly, preserving identity scaling $\tilde{x}_1 = x_1$. Two further boundary cases are worth stating: when all channels have equal variance shares ($s_k = \bar{s}$ for all $k$), every gate converges to the neutral value $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ rather than to identity---the gate is therefore a \emph{relative} reweighting whose uniform scaling is absorbed by downstream LayerNorm, not a normalized partition of weights. A near-constant channel ($s_k \to 0$) is gated toward $\sigma$ of a strongly negative standardized score ($g_k \to 0$), i.e., it is suppressed rather than removed. We verified each case numerically on the released implementation: $K{=}1$ returns weights identically $1.0$; i.i.d.\ eight-channel inputs yield gate mean $0.50 \pm 0.22$ (finite-sample spread about the neutral value); a $10\times$-variance channel gates to $0.93$ versus $0.41$ for the remainder; a constant channel gates to $0.08$; and an abrupt mid-context variance shift is detected identically to a persistent one ($0.93$), since variance shares pool over the full context window.

\paragraph{Zero-Leakage Data Provenance and Temporal Isolation}
To prevent data contamination across the workflow, we enforce strict temporal isolation across training, nominal calibration, and held-out evaluation partitions as formalized in Section~\ref{subsec:exp_setup}. Channel standardization statistics ($\bm{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \bm{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$) are computed exclusively on the training partition; the calibration partition is used only to fit extreme-value tail parameters ($u, \sigma, \xi$) with model weights and standardization statistics frozen; and scoring on the evaluation partition proceeds chronologically with frozen weights and thresholds $\tau_{\text{eff}}$. At each inference step $t$, context $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t-C:t, :]$ and suspect horizon $x_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t:t+S, :]$ maintain strict causal separation. When the auxiliary input-channel gate is enabled, its weights $\mathbf{g}$ depend exclusively on $x_{\text{ctx}}$, ensuring zero target leakage ($\partial g_k / \partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$); the deployed pipeline weights inputs uniformly.

\paragraph{Gating Boundary Conditions and Failure Modes}
We note three operational boundary conditions inherent to cross-channel variance gating. First, if a sensor channel exhibits intrinsically small normal fluctuations (such as stable thermal or cryogenic telemetry), its variance share $s_k$ remains low, attenuating its contribution even when an anomaly manifests as an abnormal variance increase that remains small in absolute magnitude relative to noisy channels. To mitigate baseline scale disparity across heterogeneous physical sensors, all raw telemetry streams are standardized via nominal calibration statistics ($\bm{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \bm{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$) prior to gating. Second, quiet faults (such as sensor flatlines, stuck values, or loss-of-signal failures where variance drops toward zero, $v_k^{\text{ctx}} \to 0$) receive low variance scores and cannot be isolated through variance amplification. Our controlled fault-type study (Section~\ref{subsec:gate_fault_study}) quantifies this blind spot empirically: single-channel flatline and stuck-sensor faults are weakly detected under every gating regime (point recall $0.02\text{--}0.07$ across modes), indicating that the limitation lies in the latent discrepancy signal on wide telemetry rather than in gating alone. Flatlines are instead detected through trajectory predictive discrepancy $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}}$, where the predictor fails to anticipate an unnatural constant state, and this pathway is partially effective only when the faulted channel contributes detectably to the aggregate latent residual. Third, abrupt operational mode transitions (e.g., thruster firings or day-to-night orbital transitions) produce transient cross-channel heteroscedasticity that temporarily perturbs variance shares until the sliding context window $C=256$ is replenished with observations from the new regime.

\subsubsection{Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Physical anomalies manifest across disparate temporal scales, ranging from high-frequency sensor spikes to gradual thermal drifts. To capture multi-scale dynamics without increasing parameter count, the encoder front-end decomposes input sequences into $J=4$ dyadic frequency bands. While continuous wavelet theory defines the Littlewood-Paley partition of unity as:
\begin{equation}
    |\phi(\omega)|^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{J-1} |\psi(2^j \omega)|^2 = 1, \quad \forall \omega \in [-\pi, \pi],
\end{equation}
discrete numerical implementations require exact partition enforcement on a finite frequency grid. Over an $N_{\text{fft}} = 256$ discrete real fast Fourier transform (rFFT) grid yielding $F = 129$ non-negative frequency bins $\omega_m = m / N_{\text{fft}}$ for $m \in \{0, \dots, N_{\text{fft}}/2\}$, dyadic center frequencies are positioned at $c_j = 2^{j - J + 1}$ for $j \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. The unnormalized shells are constructed in three branches: a half-Gaussian profile anchored at DC for $j = 0$, log-frequency octave bells $\tilde{\Psi}_j(\omega_m) = \exp\left(-\frac{(\log_2 \omega_m - \log_2 c_j)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ with bandwidth $\sigma = 0.6$ for interior shells $j \in \{1, 2\}$, and a plateau rising toward the Nyquist edge for $j = J-1$. To strictly preserve the partition property across all discrete frequencies, filter weights are normalized bin-wise into an exact partition of unity:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_j[\omega_m] = \frac{\tilde{\Psi}_j(\omega_m)}{\sum_{l=0}^{J-1} \tilde{\Psi}_l(\omega_m)}, \quad \sum_{j=0}^{J-1} \Psi_j[\omega_m] \equiv 1.0, \quad \forall m \in \{0, \dots, F-1\}.
\end{equation}
The frequency-domain split is applied directly via $\mathbf{x}_j = \mathrm{irfft}(\Psi_j \odot \mathrm{rfft}(\mathbf{x}))$, which is numerically exact: the measured partition-of-unity deviation is below $1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ and end-to-end reconstruction error is below $5 \times 10^{-8}$. Each separated shell is then processed by a full (non-depthwise) causal 1D convolution with kernel sizes $k \in \{15, 9, 5, 3\}$ from the coarsest to the finest octave, applying left-side causal padding of $p = k - 1$ steps with zero right-side padding to prevent temporal leakage across window boundaries. A learned softmax scale router recombines the shells using pooled per-shell energy descriptors, followed by a $1 \times 1$ output projection, a residual connection scaled by $\gamma = 0.1$, and LayerNorm. Because the normalized shells overlap smoothly at band edges, their band energies are correlated ($\sum_j \|\mathbf{x}_j\|^2$ recovers approximately $70\%$ of the signal energy), and the finest shell concentrates near the Nyquist bin; we report these measured properties rather than claim ideal octave separation. To quantify how the realized partition compares with a standard multiresolution analysis, we evaluated the fraction of each band's spectral mass falling inside its ideal dyadic octave $[2^{-(j+1)}, 2^{-j}]$ against the Mallat-form octave-band responses of orthonormal Haar and Daubechies-4 wavelet filter banks (\texttt{reports/lp\_filterbank\_diagnostics.csv}). Our normalized shells place $\{0.50, 0.33, 0.20, {\approx}1.00\}$ of their mass inside the corresponding ideal octaves, versus $\{0.78, 0.50, 0.55, 0.82\}$ for Haar and $\{0.86, 0.63, 0.64, 0.87\}$ for Daubechies-4: the implemented front-end is \emph{less} frequency-localized than either standard wavelet bank, trading octave isolation for smooth, exactly-reconstructing amplitude partitions and for learned per-shell scale routing. We therefore characterize the block as a Fourier-domain multiscale front-end with an exact discrete partition of unity---not as an exact Littlewood-Paley octave decomposition---and rely on the matched on/off ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablations} (Table~\ref{tab:ablations}c) to measure its empirical contribution. This decomposition isolates transient high-frequency perturbations from low-frequency diurnal baselines before latent projection.

\subsubsection{Kurtosis-Adaptive Extreme Value Theory (EVT) and Calibration Realities}
Following scoring, binary alarm decisions $\hat{y}_t = \mathbb{I}(\mathcal{S}(t) > \tau_t)$ are generated via Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}. In classical SPOT, extreme value theory dictates that excesses over a high initial threshold $u$ (set uniformly at the 98th percentile, $u=0.98$) asymptotically follow a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) with scale $\sigma$ and shape $\xi$:
\begin{equation}
    \tau = u + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\left[ \left(\frac{q N}{N_u}\right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right],
\end{equation}
where $q = 10^{-3}$ denotes the design risk probability, $N$ is total calibration samples, and $N_u$ is the count of excesses. To account for varying tail heaviness across diverse sensor regimes, we modulate the threshold via an empirical heuristic factor governed by the sample excess kurtosis $\gamma_2$ of calibration residuals:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau \cdot \left[ 1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh(\gamma_2) \right].
\end{equation}
On heavy-tailed distributions ($\gamma_2 > 0$), $\tau_{\text{eff}}$ increases, raising the threshold to suppress false alarms from volatile noise bursts; on light-tailed distributions ($\gamma_2 < 0$), the threshold tightens to maximize detection sensitivity.

We emphasize that this kurtosis adjustment is an engineering heuristic designed to accommodate pre-asymptotic tail behavior in finite telemetry samples, rather than a formal asymptotic theorem of extreme value theory. In our 16-cell protocol ablation (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} and Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}), isolating the kurtosis multiplier revealed that it does not improve out-of-sample risk control: on held-out data, modulating the threshold by $1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh(\gamma_2)$ increased nominal FPR (e.g., for TS-JEPA from $6.51\%$ to $22.05\%$) without a statistically distinguishable change in Point-F1 ($0.2113$ vs. $0.1968$). 

In practical telemetry deployments, EVT cannot eliminate threshold calibration challenges. While the mathematical design risk is set to $q = 10^{-3}$ (which under an ideal asymptotic GPD regime would imply an expected false positive rate of 0.1\%), empirical false positive rates across real-world multi-sensor streams typically settle around $5\text{--}8\%$, representing a substantial calibration gap. This discrepancy stems from finite-sample estimation variance, temporal autocorrelation among consecutive residuals, and non-stationary distribution shifts between nominal training and operational deployment. Under thresholds fit strictly on held-out nominal data (Section~\ref{sec:exp}), the measured nominal FPR at $q=10^{-3}$ is $6.5\text{--}7.7\%$ for the JEPAs and $26\text{--}27\%$ for TimesNet and TranAD, demonstrating that EVT does not deliver its design risk for any model and that the kurtosis adjustment does not close the gap. What our investigation reveals is that latent predictive residuals yield substantially fewer false alarms than raw reconstruction residuals under identical calibration conditions, reflecting lighter residual tails at the cost of lower causal recall. In our benchmark, thresholds are calibrated using held-out nominal splits under static EVT; in operational incident analysis, dynamic rolling SPOT is evaluated to examine streaming baseline drift.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Hyperparameters}
\label{subsec:exp_setup}
To ensure rigorous and fair comparison, all architectures are trained and evaluated under an identical streaming time-series pipeline. Telemetry streams are processed using a sliding window with past context duration $C=256$ and suspect target horizon $S=64$ (total window length $L=320$), evaluated with a stride of 1 timestep across all testing streams.

Context representations $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ ($D=32$) are extracted using a causal dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) comprising 3 residual blocks with channel depth $F=48$, kernel size $K=5$, and dilation factors $d \in \{1, 2, 4\}$. While the convolutional receptive field across the 3 dilated blocks spans $1 + 2(K-1)\sum_l d_l = 57$ timesteps, global temporal average pooling across the resulting feature maps aggregates information across the entire context window $C=256$, condensing the sequence into a fixed-dimensional latent representation $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ regularized by Layer Normalization and dropout ($p=0.20$). The momentum target encoder parameters $\theta_{\text{tgt}}$ are updated via Exponential Moving Average (EMA) tracking: $\theta_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow m \theta_{\text{tgt}} + (1-m) \theta_{\text{ctx}}$, where momentum coefficient $m$ follows a cosine schedule from $m_{\text{base}}=0.996$ to $m_{\text{max}}=0.9995$.

Models are optimized using AdamW with an initial learning rate $\eta=10^{-3}$ decayed to $\eta_{\text{min}}=10^{-5}$ via Cosine Annealing, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and mini-batch size $B=32$ across 20 training epochs on nominal telemetry sequences. 

\paragraph{Held-Out Calibration Protocol}
To evaluate out-of-sample threshold calibration without circularity or test-label leakage, we partition each stream's nominal telemetry into three strictly disjoint chronological blocks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training Block (first 65\%):} Used exclusively to train neural network representations and feature extraction weights.
    \item \textbf{Nominal Calibration Block (subsequent 17.5\%):} Completely withheld from model training and hyperparameter tuning. Used exclusively to compute nominal residual scores and fit stream-specific extreme-value tail parameters (Generalized Pareto Distribution scale $\sigma$ and shape $\xi$ above the 98th percentile $u=0.98$ under design risk $q=10^{-3}$). Tail parameters are fitted independently per stream and per model architecture. Sample kurtosis $\gamma_2$ is estimated strictly within this calibration split.
    \item \textbf{Nominal Evaluation Block (final 17.5\%):} Separated from the calibration block by an unobserved 64-step isolation gap to prevent window overlap. On this block, the empirical False Positive Rate (Held-Out FPR) is measured on nominal data the threshold never encountered during calibration.
\end{enumerate}
In the primary benchmark, thresholds remain fixed during test evaluation to measure the stability of static extreme-value bounds under operational distribution drift. In operational incident analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:incident_metrics}), dynamic rolling SPOT is evaluated to examine adaptive tracking of baseline drift.

\paragraph{Calibrator Assignment Across Experiments}
To avoid ambiguity between the calibration variants appearing throughout this paper, we specify the exact calibrator used in each experiment. \textbf{Primary held-out benchmark} (Tables~\ref{tab:core_benchmark}, \ref{tab:causal_benchmark}, and \ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}; all paired statistics in Section~\ref{sec:results}): standard SPOT ($u = p_{98}$, $q = 10^{-3}$, no kurtosis multiplier) fit on the nominal calibration block; the headline metrics correspond to the ``SPOT'' column of Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}, with the kurtosis-adjusted, empirical-quantile, and validation-maximum variants evaluated on identical score streams for comparison only. \textbf{Matched-backbone controls, latent-space controls, gating-regime ablations, dyadic front-end ablation, and fault-type study} (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}): SPOT with the $\tanh(\gamma_2)/6$ kurtosis multiplier, as implemented by the \texttt{adaptive\_kurtosis} flag in the released scripts. \textbf{Early-warning and EVT mechanism studies} (Sections~\ref{subsec:causal_vs_buffered} and \ref{subsec:calibration_realities}): standard SPOT unless the calibrator is itself the object of comparison (risk sweep, declustering, drift analyses), in which case both variants are reported explicitly. \textbf{Incident metrics} (Section~\ref{subsec:incident_metrics}): rolling SPOT as noted. No experiment silently mixes calibrators across models; within each table all models share the same calibration rule.

\paragraph{Multi-Seed Replication, Primary Endpoints, and Multiplicity Controls}
To account for initialization variance, all principal learned models on the 19 non-GHL benchmark streams are trained across five independent random seeds (42, 123, 456, 7, and 2024); the twelve large GHL streams (each exceeding $10^{6}$ observations) are evaluated across up to three seeds owing to training cost, and controlled-ablation controls (e.g., $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$, $\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$, no-regime) use three seeds. We define two co-primary hypothesis endpoints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Nominal False-Positive Rate Reduction:} Evaluated via out-of-sample held-out nominal FPR to test whether latent prediction stabilizes automated EVT bounds against false-alarm flooding.
    \item \textbf{Detection Parity:} Evaluated via unadjusted Point-F1 and continuous ranking PR-AUC to test whether false-alarm suppression is achieved without degrading discriminative detection.
\end{enumerate}
For statistical inference, we report mean performance, seed standard deviations, moving-block bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (block length $L=64$, preserving temporal autocorrelation within continuous streams), and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired stream-level differences. The independent unit of analysis is the telemetry stream ($n = 19$ non-GHL streams for the five-seed principal comparison, $n = 33$ where GHL streams enter at their available seed coverage): random seeds are averaged within each stream before pairing, and repeated sliding windows within a stream are aggregated to a single stream-level score per model, so no window or seed is counted twice. Because nonsignificant tests do not establish equivalence, we make no noninferiority claim: all paired differences are reported with effect sizes and confidence intervals so that readers can judge practical margins directly. To control the family-wise error rate across multiple pairwise comparisons against baselines and ablation controls, $p$-values are adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure with nominal rate $q_{\text{FDR}} = 0.05$. Paired differences where the 95\% bootstrap confidence interval spans zero or adjusted $p > 0.05$ are treated as statistically inconclusive rather than evidence of equivalence.

\subsection{Baseline Architectures and Parameter Capacities}
To isolate algorithmic principles from external implementation discrepancies, all baselines are executed within a unified PyTorch benchmarking pipeline under identical windowing, standardization, and EVT calibration:

\noindent\textbf{TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}:} A multi-periodic reconstructive architecture that transforms 1D time series into 2D variation spaces via 1D-FFT periodic frequency discovery and 2D Inception blocks ($648{,}553$ trainable parameters at benchmark dimension $K=25$; $574{,}529$ at $K=9$).

\noindent\textbf{TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad}:} An adversarial reconstructive Transformer featuring two-stage self-conditioning and sub-series attention ($205{,}746$ trainable parameters at $K=25$; $201{,}090$ at $K=9$).

\noindent\textbf{TS-JEPA (Unconstrained Control):} A standard Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture predicting target representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from context $z_{\text{ctx}}$ via a 2-layer MLP predictor without physical inductive priors ($289{,}061$ trainable parameters at $K=25$; $218{,}309$ at $K=9$). Across varying channel dimensions $K$, only the initial 1D projection layer scales ($+48$ parameters per channel), while the Littlewood-Paley front-end employs fixed dyadic partition coefficients and channel-independent per-shell filters.

\noindent\textbf{Physics-Inspired Formulations:} Three candidate architectures incorporating spectral Koopman dissipation (\texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, $285{,}733$ parameters at $K=25$; $214{,}981$ at $K=9$), turbulent momentum transport (\texttt{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, $295{,}953$ parameters at $K=25$; $225{,}201$ at $K=9$), and conservative curl-free potential constraints (\texttt{Potential-Flow JEPA}, $338{,}117$ parameters at $K=25$; $267{,}365$ at $K=9$). In \texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, parameterizing the linear Koopman transition $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}$ directly via matrix $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ requires $D^2 = 1{,}024$ weights, replacing the MLP predictor and reducing overall capacity ($1.15\%$ smaller than TS-JEPA at $K=25$, and $1.5\%$ smaller at $K=9$). Reynolds-Stress introduces $+2.4\%$ parameters at $K=25$ for its covariance closure head, while Potential-Flow adds $+17.0\%$ parameters through its velocity-matching potential network. These specifications confirm that performance characteristics across models do not stem from parameter scaling discrepancies.

\noindent\textbf{Linear AR Forecaster (Non-Neural Baseline):} A classical vector autoregressive baseline that fits a ridge-regularized linear mapping from flattened context windows $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \cdot K}$ directly to target windows $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \cdot K}$ in observation space ($C=256, S=64$). Anomaly scores are computed from raw observation-space prediction errors $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{tgt}} - \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}\|_2^2$ and calibrated using the identical held-out EVT/SPOT procedure. This non-neural control isolates whether representation learning in latent space is necessary, or whether linear dynamics suffice for residual conditioning.

\subsection{Multi-Tier Statistical Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:eval_metrics}
Recent critical surveys in time-series anomaly detection \cite{kim2022towards, schmidl2022anomaly, wu2023current} demonstrate that standard evaluation protocols are plagued by severe reporting vulnerabilities, including point-adjustment inflation, channel-averaging imbalances, and arbitrary threshold gaming. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, we report evaluation metrics across multiple complementary tiers:

\noindent\textbf{Dataset-Macro Point-F1 (Primary Metric):} We compute the unweighted arithmetic mean of Point-F1 scores across datasets:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Macro-F1}_{\text{DS}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \left( \frac{1}{|C_d|} \sum_{c \in C_d} \text{F1}_{c} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of benchmark datasets and $C_d$ is the set of channels in dataset $d$. This prevents datasets with numerous channels (such as GHL with 14 streams) from dominating the aggregate benchmark score, giving equal weight to each distinct physical application domain.

\noindent\textbf{Pooled Micro Point-F1:} We pool all raw True Positives, False Positives, and False Negatives globally across all test timesteps:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Micro-F1} = \frac{2 \sum_{c} \text{TP}_c}{2 \sum_c \text{TP}_c + \sum_c \text{FP}_c + \sum_c \text{FN}_c}
\end{equation}
Micro-F1 directly penalizes models that flood long streams with hundreds of thousands of false alarms, exposing reconstructive noise drowning.

\noindent\textbf{Channel-Macro Point-F1:} The standard per-channel average $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{F1}_i$ across all evaluated channels.

\noindent\textbf{Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC):} Average Precision computed over all possible continuous anomaly score thresholds:
\begin{equation}
    \text{PR-AUC} = \sum_{k} (R_k - R_{k-1}) P_k
\end{equation}
PR-AUC evaluates raw score ranking capability entirely independent of the threshold calibration mechanism.

\noindent\textbf{Event Recall (Segment-Level Recall):} In real operational telemetry, an anomaly event consists of a contiguous interval of faulty timestamps $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$. An event is successfully detected if the model triggers at least one alarm during the segment: $\exists t \in [t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ such that $\hat{y}_t = 1$. The Event Recall denotes the fraction of ground-truth anomaly events successfully captured across the benchmark.

\noindent\textbf{Mean Detection Delay ($\Delta t$):} For each successfully detected anomaly segment $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$, the detection delay is the number of elapsed timesteps between fault onset and the earliest alarm: $\Delta t = \min\{t \in [t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}] : \hat{y}_t = 1\} - t_{\text{start}}$. The Mean Detection Delay averages $\Delta t$ across all detected events. Undetected events are reflected in the Event Recall rather than assigned an arbitrary latency penalty.

\noindent\textbf{Trivial All-Positive Reference Baseline:} We report the analytical performance of a constant all-positive predictor ($\hat{y}_t = 1, \forall t$):
\begin{equation}
    \text{F1}_{\text{all-pos}} = \frac{2 \cdot P}{2 \cdot P + N} = \frac{2 \pi}{1 + \pi}
\end{equation}
where $\pi = P / (P+N)$ is the anomaly base rate. Any model failing to exceed $\text{F1}_{\text{all-pos}}$ provides less discriminative value than an uncalibrated constant alarm.

\noindent\textbf{Contiguous Point-Adjusted F1 (PA-F1):} Standard segment adjustment where detecting any single anomaly point expands to the entire contiguous anomaly segment. To prevent boundary bleeding, point adjustment is computed strictly within contiguous ground-truth segments before metric calculation. We also report the \textit{Test-Sweep PA-F1} (best post-hoc threshold scan) to quantify the remaining calibration gap.

\subsection{Evaluation Regimes: Zero-Lookahead Causal vs. Buffered Inference}
\label{subsec:causal_vs_buffered_protocol}
A critical operational consideration in streaming telemetry monitoring is the temporal availability of observations at decision time. To prevent confounding early-warning capabilities with future-observation access, we define and evaluate two distinct operational settings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Strictly Causal Online Detection (Zero Lookahead):} At decision timestamp $T$, the detector accesses strictly historical observations up to the current time $\{X_\tau\}_{\tau \le T}$. Scores are mapped via trailing attribution, guaranteeing that alarms are generated with zero lookahead latency budget ($\text{Latency} = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Buffered Window Detection ($S=64$ Lookahead Delay):} An observation buffer of $S=64$ future timesteps is collected before evaluating target predictive consistency. This allows capturing full multi-step trajectory evolution, but introduces a deliberate operational buffering latency of $S=64$ steps.
\end{enumerate}
For both settings, we explicitly record Mean Detection Delay ($\Delta t = t_{\text{alarm}} - t_{\text{onset}}$ in timesteps from ground-truth fault inception), Event Recall within delay budgets ($\Delta t \le 10, 64$ steps), and False Alarm Rate per 1,000 timesteps ($\text{FAR}_{1\text{k}}$).

\section{Ablation Studies and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablations}

To isolate the individual contributions of each mathematical and architectural component, we conduct controlled ablation experiments across multiple telemetry regimes.

\subsection{Ablation 1: Physical Regularizer Controls on Fixed Architectures}
\label{subsec:controlled_ablations}
To test whether the proposed physics-inspired regularizers provide measurable benefits over unconstrained latent prediction, Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) reports controlled ablations across all 19 non-GHL benchmark streams spanning six physical domains (Daphnet, MSL, SMAP, Genesis, Metro, and SWAN; principal models evaluated across five seeds and ablation controls across three seeds under the held-out calibration protocol). In each comparison, the neural backbone is held strictly constant while the specific physical loss term or inductive module is disabled:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the spectral entropy regularizer ($\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$) leaves Point-F1 unchanged (three-seed matched pairing: $0.241 \pm 0.014$ vs. $0.244 \pm 0.024$, difference $-0.003$, $95\%$ bootstrap interval $[-0.011, +0.005]$, Wilcoxon $p=0.62$). Both variants maintain PR-AUC around $0.347\text{--}0.348$.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. $\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the second-moment stress loss ($\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$) yields $0.203 \pm 0.059$ Point-F1 compared to $0.208 \pm 0.071$ with the stress loss active (difference $+0.005$, interval $[-0.006, +0.016]$, $p=0.41$). Neither difference exceeds random seed variation ($p = 0.41$). The result is also insensitive to the covariance estimator itself: replacing the instantaneous rank-1 outer product $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}}$ with a per-sample temporal covariance across the target horizon (\texttt{Reynolds-tempcov}) or a batch-level covariance (\texttt{Reynolds-batchcov}) on nine non-GHL streams (three seeds) shifts Point-F1 by $-0.009$ and $-0.010$ and held-out FPR by $+0.004$ and $-0.042$ respectively --- none materially different. The rank-1 construction is therefore not the reason the regularizer fails to help; the second-moment term appears to carry no signal beyond ordinary latent prediction under either statistically richer estimator.
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. No-Regime Control:} Disabling multi-subspace regime routing ($K_r=1$) yields $0.129 \pm 0.061$ Point-F1 compared to $0.096 \pm 0.026$ with routing active (difference $-0.033$, interval $[-0.060, -0.010]$, $p=0.048$). Both underperform unconstrained TS-JEPA ($0.234 \pm 0.030$ at five seeds, $p=0.013$).
\end{enumerate}
These controlled ablations across the full 19-stream suite demonstrate that the physical regularizers do not produce statistically detectable improvements over unconstrained latent prediction on telemetry.

\subsection{Ablation 2: Auxiliary Input-Channel Gating Variant in High-Dimensional Sensor Arrays}
In high-dimensional telemetry ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults typically originate in a localized subsystem (e.g., a single valve or battery cell). In standard latent pooling:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\text{pool}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K z_k
\end{equation}
the localized anomalous signal is diluted by ambient normal noise across the remaining quiescent channels. The deployed pipeline mitigates this dilution in latent space via the coordinate-saliency gate on predictive residuals (Section~\ref{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}); input channels are encoded unweighted. As an auxiliary variant, we separately evaluate the causal input-channel variance gate $\tilde{x}_k = x_k \cdot \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right)$ applied symmetrically at training and scoring time. Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b) compares this variant against uniform input weighting across four multivariate streams.

\paragraph{Causal Leakage and Preprocessing Ablation}
To characterize the input-gating variant without target-dependent leakage, we train four gating regimes under an identical TS-JEPA configuration ($C=256, S=64$, 10 epochs, seed 42, SPOT $q=10^{-3}$ calibrated on held-out nominal telemetry):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shared Context-Derived Gating:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ are computed strictly from historical context and applied symmetrically to both context and target windows, guaranteeing strict causality ($\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$).
    \item \textbf{Target-Only Leaky Control:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ are computed from the suspect target window. This non-causal control artificially amplifies channels that spike within the target window and upper-bounds the benefit obtainable by violating temporal causality.
    \item \textbf{Independent Gating:} Computing $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ for context and $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ for target applies asymmetric coordinate transformations, breaking latent metric alignment.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Pooling (Deployed):} Input channels are encoded unweighted; this regime coincides with the deployed pipeline, in which dimensional drowning is controlled only by the latent coordinate-saliency gate.
\end{enumerate}
The measured regime comparison is reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b). The effect of input-channel gating is stream-dependent in both sign and magnitude: the context-derived gate substantially improves MSL ($0.047 \to 0.227$), where anomalies concentrate in few coordinates against many quiescent ones, but degrades SMAP ($0.280 \to 0.019$), where fault signatures are distributed across channels and variance weighting attenuates informative coordinates. Under macro-averaged Point-F1, uniform input weighting ($0.118$) outperforms the context-derived gate ($0.077$), while the non-causal target-only control is slightly higher ($0.128$); independent context/target gating is weakest ($0.071$). Because the leaky control captures only a small additional margin ($+8.6\%$ relative over causal gating), most of the gating effect is attributable to quiescent-noise suppression rather than target-window statistics. We therefore retain uniform input weighting in the deployed pipeline and report input-channel gating as an auxiliary variant whose benefit depends on the anomaly's cross-channel dispersion profile.

\paragraph{Fault-Type Sensitivity of Channel Gating}
\label{subsec:gate_fault_study}
To characterize per-fault-type behavior, we inject six single-channel fault classes (flatline, stuck sensor, dropout, low-amplitude step, spike control, and mid-variance flatline) into six multivariate streams and evaluate four gating modes. Two findings emerge. First, the context-derived variance gate approximately doubles point recall relative to uniform pooling (macro $0.045$ vs.\ $0.023$) at roughly three times the nominal false-positive rate ($0.069$ vs.\ $0.019$); a robust-MAD variant is marginally stronger ($0.060$ recall). Second, the increase is concentrated on transient signatures, while quiet faults (flatline, stuck sensor, dropout) remain weakly detected under \textit{every} gating regime (point recall $0.02\text{--}0.07$). The blind spot therefore reflects the aggregate latent discrepancy signal on wide telemetry rather than a deficiency of gating alone, and input-channel gating is reported as an evaluated variant with a precision--recall trade-off rather than adopted in the deployed pipeline.

\subsection{Ablation 3: Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Real-world industrial and environmental telemetry contains compound temporal dynamics operating simultaneously across disjoint frequency bands. For instance, in municipal water infrastructure (\texttt{GECCO}), sensors record 24-hour diurnal consumption cycles alongside sudden pressure drops and pipe contaminant inflows across multi-hour horizons.

Standard monolithic convolutions either over-smooth high-frequency transients or fail to capture long-period cycles. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(c), replacing the standard convolutional front-end with the Fourier-domain dyadic shell decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_j = \mathrm{irfft}(\Psi_j \odot \mathrm{rfft}(\mathbf{x})), \quad j \in \{0, 1, \dots, J-1\}
\end{equation}
followed by per-shell causal convolutions and learned scale routing, produces a modest and stream-dependent effect under an otherwise identical \texttt{TS-JEPA} configuration. Across five multivariate streams, the dyadic front-end improves macro-averaged buffered Point-F1 from $0.088$ to $0.131$ ($+49.6\%$ relative, $+0.044$ absolute), with gains concentrated on MSL ($0.002 \to 0.052$) and SMD ($0.000 \to 0.177$), a small regression on SMAP ($0.294 \to 0.261$), and near-zero detection on \texttt{GECCO} under both configurations. Two caveats apply: enabling the block adds approximately $1.6 \times 10^5$ parameters, so part of the gain may reflect capacity rather than frequency separation, and the measured shell overlap (Section~\ref{sec:proposed_method}) means the decomposition does not attain ideal octave isolation. The dyadic front-end is retained as a modest contributor, not the primary driver of the latent-residual conditioning effect.

\subsection{Ablation 4: Threshold Calibration Rules on Nominal Splits}
\label{subsec:ablation_calibration}
The complete calibrator comparison across all 33 streams and all learned architectures is reported in Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} and Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}; we summarize the two findings most relevant to the ablation suite here. First, simple non-parametric rules match parametric SPOT: on TS-JEPA, a validation-maximum rule attains held-out FPR $0.0686$ versus $0.0651$ for standard SPOT at nearly identical Point-F1. Second, the kurtosis-adaptive multiplier degrades calibration, inflating TS-JEPA held-out FPR from $0.0651$ to $0.2205$ while leaving Point-F1 statistically indistinguishable ($0.2113$ vs.\ $0.1968$). Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} decomposes this failure into extremal clustering, chronological drift, and GPD goodness-of-fit breakdown.

\subsection{Controlled Backbone Comparison: Latent Space vs. Observation Space}
\label{subsec:controlled_backbone}
A foundational hypothesis of this investigation is that predicting dynamics in a contracted latent representation space produces residual distributions that are fundamentally better conditioned for automated extreme-value thresholding than residuals derived from raw observation-space reconstruction or forecasting. However, directly contrasting full-featured JEPA models against existing public baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD) introduces potential architectural confounding from differing receptive fields, encoder depths, normalization schemes, and pooling operations.

To eliminate these confounding factors, Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} evaluates three architectural formulations under a strictly unified \texttt{HybridTCNEncoder} backbone ($C=256, S=64$, latent dimension $d=32$, 3-layer dilated causal TCN with 48 filters, LayerNorm, GELU, and temporal pooling):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Recon:} Autoencoder encoding the full window $X_{t-C:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{(C+S)\times K}$ into $z \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding back to reconstruct the observation space $\hat{X}$. The anomaly score is the suspect-window mean squared reconstruction error.
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Pred:} Forecaster encoding context $X_{t-C:t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ into $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding directly to forecast upcoming observations $\hat{X}_{t:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$. The anomaly score is the raw observation forecasting residual.
    \item \textbf{TS-JEPA (Latent Space):} Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture encoding context to $z_{\text{ctx}}$, predicting the future latent representation $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = g_\phi(z_{\text{ctx}})$, and scoring via predictive discrepancy against target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from an EMA target encoder.
\end{enumerate}
All models are trained for 10 epochs using AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$) with batch size 16 and calibrated via Kurtosis-Adaptive EVT ($u=0.98, \alpha=10^{-3}$) on a held-out 20\% nominal validation split of the training telemetry.

\noindent\textbf{Observation Reconstruction vs. Observation Forecasting:} A central question is whether the reduction in false alarms stems from forecasting upcoming dynamics rather than reconstructing full windows, or whether it requires representation in latent space. The matched-backbone results in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} resolve this question. Comparing TCN-Obs-Recon and TCN-Obs-Pred reveals virtually identical tail statistics and false-positive rates: validation kurtosis is $6.09$ vs.\ $6.00$, test kurtosis is $57.26$ vs.\ $55.66$, and clean empirical FPR is $27.29\%$ vs.\ $27.57\%$. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across the 15 streams confirm that observation reconstruction and observation forecasting are statistically indistinguishable in clean FPR ($p = 0.6784$) and Point-F1 ($p = 1.0000$). Forecasting in raw observation space does not condition residual tails because residuals directly reflect sensor jitter and point-level noise.

\noindent\textbf{Latent Space Projection and Tail Compression:} Shifting the predictive loss to latent representation space (\texttt{TS-JEPA}) fundamentally alters the residual distribution. Test excess kurtosis drops from $55.66$ to $1.77$ (a $>31\times$ reduction in tail weight), producing near-Gaussian residuals. Under held-out EVT calibration, this tail compression significantly lowers the clean empirical false-positive rate from $27.57\%$ to $11.80\%$ (Wilcoxon $p = 0.0479$), eliminating over $6,500$ false alarms per stream. Under severe noise injection ($\text{SNR}=0\text{ dB}$), observation-space models experience a catastrophic failure in threshold calibration, with FPR surging past $50\%$ ($54.10\%$ for reconstruction, $50.98\%$ for forecasting). In contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} maintains an empirical FPR of $13.93\%$ ($p = 0.0026$), confirming that latent representations provide stability against external sensor perturbations.

\noindent\textbf{The Operational Sensitivity Trade-Off:} The tail stability of latent prediction incurs a measurable operational trade-off in point-level detection sensitivity. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone}, clean Point-F1 declines from $0.1916$ for TCN-Obs-Pred to $0.0785$ for TS-JEPA (Wilcoxon $p = 0.0054$), with point recall dropping from $0.4315$ to $0.1460$. Because the latent encoder condenses multi-channel sequences into semantic trajectory vectors, localized point anomalies that do not perturb macroscopic state evolution are filtered alongside noise. Latent prediction thus operates as a conservative state detector, suppressing false alarms at the price of reduced sensitivity to subtle, isolated point faults.

\noindent\textbf{Investigation of Native Window Horizons:} A secondary hypothesis is whether reconstructive baselines suffered under automated EVT calibration because they were evaluated on long temporal windows ($W=320$) rather than their originally proposed native horizons ($W=10$ for TranAD, $W=96$ for TimesNet). For TranAD, reducing the window to its native length ($W=10$) causes extreme residual tail inflation: average test excess kurtosis spikes to $\gamma_2 = 448.25$ (a $15\times$ increase over $W=320$, reaching $\gamma_2 = 809.32$ on GECCO and $781.44$ on SMAP). Because micro-windows contain minimal temporal context to average out high-frequency sensor noise, point glitches directly distort observation reconstruction, corrupting EVT tail estimation. Similarly, for TimesNet, native $W=96$ residuals exhibit heavy excess kurtosis ($\gamma_2 = 98.14$) and an average empirical false positive rate of $44.09\%$, generating over $26,500$ false alarms per stream. By contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} maintains well-conditioned residuals ($\gamma_2 = 3.14$) and achieves the lowest empirical false positive rate ($22.57\%$). Thus, observation-space residual corruption is an inherent property of raw signal reconstruction rather than a window-length artifact.

Several primary empirical findings emerge from Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark}:

\noindent\textbf{False Alarm Suppression on Held-Out Telemetry:} The primary operational advantage of latent-space predictive architectures is a substantial reduction in nominal false alarms. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves an out-of-sample nominal FPR of $0.076$, Operator-Entropy achieves $0.086$, and Reynolds-Stress achieves $0.078$, compared to $0.300$ for TimesNet and $0.307$ for TranAD. Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), paired difference tests establish a statistically significant reduction in held-out FPR: TS-JEPA achieves a mean paired FPR reduction of $-0.150$ against TimesNet ($95\%$ bootstrap confidence interval $[-0.245, -0.067]$, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.007$) and $-0.150$ against TranAD ($[-0.246, -0.070]$, $p=0.004$). Similarly, Operator-Entropy achieves a paired FPR reduction of $-0.146$ against TimesNet ($[-0.236, -0.068]$, $p=0.003$) and $-0.146$ against TranAD ($[-0.245, -0.060]$, $p=0.002$). Predicting residuals in contracted latent representations avoids observation-level high-frequency noise, yielding better-conditioned score distributions (as isolated directly under the same-backbone ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_backbone} and Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone}).

\noindent\textbf{Point-F1 and Continuous Ranking (PR-AUC) Parity:} While latent prediction suppresses nominal false alarms, it does not provide a statistically distinguishable improvement in overall detection fidelity. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves a stream-macro Point-F1 of $0.234$ and PR-AUC of $0.323$ (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.148$), compared to $0.197$ Point-F1 and $0.329$ PR-AUC for TimesNet (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.100$), and $0.130$ Point-F1 and $0.287$ PR-AUC for TranAD (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.069$). Across all 33 streams, paired differences between TS-JEPA and TimesNet are $+0.012$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ CI $[-0.053, +0.075]$, $p=0.54$) and $-0.000$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.098, +0.092]$, $p=0.39$). Against TranAD, the paired difference is $+0.051$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.015, +0.129]$, $p=0.88$) and $+0.023$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.077, +0.125]$, $p=0.16$). Thus, continuous ranking fidelity and calibrated Point-F1 remain statistically comparable between unconstrained latent prediction and observation-space reconstructive baselines.

\noindent\textbf{Event Recall and Detection Conservatism:} The lower false-positive rate of latent predictive models is accompanied by a reduction in event recall. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TimesNet captures $58.6\%$ of ground-truth anomaly events, and TranAD captures $48.1\%$, whereas TS-JEPA captures $49.8\%$, Reynolds-Stress captures $39.8\%$, and Potential-Flow captures $33.1\%$. Latent-space residuals act as a more conservative detector: by filtering high-frequency variations, subtle anomaly onsets that do not produce large latent coordinate shifts are missed, suppressing false alarms at the expense of lower event sensitivity.

\noindent\textbf{Detection Delay Trade-Offs ($\Delta t$):} Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} also reports the mean detection delay $\Delta t$ in timesteps from fault inception to the earliest alarm across detected events. Detection latency varies substantially across architectures. TimesNet responds most rapidly to raw observation anomalies, averaging $\Delta t = 48$ steps, while TS-JEPA incurs a mean delay of $80$ steps and the physics-inspired variants are slower still (Reynolds-Stress $133$ steps, Potential-Flow $147$ steps, Operator-Entropy $192$ steps). TranAD exhibits the longest delay of any evaluated model ($234$ steps). Because latent encoders smooth out high-frequency observation noise, alarms trigger only after anomalous deviations propagate into the dominant coordinates of representation space. Operational deployments must therefore weigh the roughly four-fold reduction in nominal false alarms against an increase in initial detection latency relative to the fastest baseline.

\noindent\textbf{Necessity of Nonlinear Representation Learning (Linear AR Control):} To establish whether non-neural linear dynamics suffice for residual conditioning, Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} includes a ridge-regularized Linear Autoregressive Forecaster. Under the identical held-out calibration protocol, Linear AR achieves an exceptionally low nominal FPR of $0.006$ and zero detection delay ($\Delta t = 0.0$ steps) on the events it captures. However, its calibrated Point-F1 collapses to $0.057$ (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.038$), capturing only $47.8\%$ of events with a point precision of just $4.35\%$. Because an observation-space linear model lacks the capacity to capture cross-channel interactions and temporal non-linearities, its residual scores fail to discriminate subtle operational faults from background variance. This non-neural baseline confirms that trivial linear forecasting cannot deliver viable anomaly detection, establishing that nonlinear latent representation learning is necessary to combine controlled false-alarm rates with usable detection accuracy.

\subsection{Evaluating Physical Inductive Priors: Hypothesis Testing and Null Results}
\label{subsec:eval_physics_priors}

A central hypothesis evaluated in this investigation is whether incorporating physics-inspired inductive regularizers into latent representations (Koopman spectral entropy, Reynolds stress closure, and potential-flow curl-free dynamics) improves anomaly detection performance over an unconstrained latent predictive architecture (\texttt{TS-JEPA}).

The empirical data across all 33 streams refute this hypothesis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. TS-JEPA:} Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), Operator-Entropy yields a mean paired difference of $+0.002$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ bootstrap CI $[-0.025, +0.029]$, Wilcoxon $p=0.69$) and $-0.000$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.021, +0.020]$, $p=0.49$). Held-out nominal FPR difference is $+0.004$ ($[-0.018, +0.029]$, $p=0.77$). Across 33 streams, Operator-Entropy outperforms TS-JEPA in Point-F1 on only 8 streams.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. TS-JEPA:} Reynolds-Stress yields a paired difference of $-0.015$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.043, +0.007]$, $p=0.50$) and a statistically significant reduction in PR-AUC ($-0.011$, $[-0.028, +0.007]$, $p=0.014$). Held-out FPR difference is $-0.002$ ($[-0.025, +0.024]$, $p=0.81$). Reynolds-Stress leads TS-JEPA in F1 on 10 of 33 streams.
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. TS-JEPA:} Potential-Flow performs significantly worse than unconstrained TS-JEPA on calibrated Point-F1 (paired difference $-0.084$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.162, -0.021]$, $p=0.013$), with a corresponding deficit in continuous ranking PR-AUC (paired difference $-0.072$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.136, -0.018]$, $p=0.060$). Potential-Flow outperforms TS-JEPA on only 5 of 33 streams.
\end{enumerate}

These findings align with the matched-capacity regularizer controls in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) and Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations}, where disabling each physical loss term on a fixed backbone produces changes well within seed noise. We conclude that the physical regularizers provide no measurable anomaly detection advantage over unconstrained latent prediction. Performance differences between models stem from shared representation conditioning rather than the physical loss terms.

\paragraph{Buffered Early-Warning Lead Time}
To separate prospective warning capability from detection fidelity, we measure the fraction of events first alerted before their labeled onset under buffered inference across five streams. TS-JEPA warns $12.3\%$ of events pre-onset ($7$ of $57$ pooled), with a median lead time of $180$ steps and $23.8$ false warnings per $1{,}000$ nominal steps. TimesNet warns $73.7\%$ of events pre-onset at a comparable median lead ($204$ steps) but emits $206.4$ false warnings per $1{,}000$ nominal steps; on GECCO it alerts $37$ of $37$ events early while generating $873.6$ false warnings per $1{,}000$ nominal steps, approaching a constant-alarm regime. Pre-onset coverage is therefore confounded by the underlying alarm rate: at its conservative operating point, TS-JEPA trades early-warning coverage for an order-of-magnitude lower nuisance-alarm rate.

\subsection{Automated Threshold Calibration Realities on Non-Stationary Telemetry}
\label{subsec:calibration_realities}

A critical requirement for autonomous telemetry monitoring is whether automated extreme-value thresholding adheres to theoretical design risk parameters. When practitioners specify a design risk of $q=10^{-3}$ ($0.1\%$), the operational expectation is that nominal false alarms will occur at or below this frequency.

\input{sections/tab_calibrator_comparison.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison} reports a controlled comparison of threshold calibrators evaluated on identical score streams across the 33 continuous telemetry streams under buffered inference. The empirical results demonstrate several key realities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic Design-Risk Overshoot:} Extreme-value thresholding via SPOT ($u=0.98, q=10^{-3}$) systematically fails to achieve its design risk on out-of-sample nominal telemetry. Across all 33 streams, empirical nominal FPR averages $6.51\%$ for TS-JEPA, $7.33\%$ for Operator-Entropy, $26.09\%$ for TimesNet, and $26.55\%$ for TranAD, overshooting the theoretical $0.1\%$ target by $65$-fold to $266$-fold.
    \item \textbf{High Failure Rate Across Streams:} The 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals for held-out nominal FPR strictly exclude the theoretical design risk $q=10^{-3}$ in $64\%$ of TS-JEPA runs, $66\%$ of Operator-Entropy runs, $87\%$ of TimesNet runs, and $84\%$ of TranAD runs. Real-world telemetry exhibits non-stationary baseline drift and seasonal fluctuations that violate the stationary tail assumptions of Extreme Value Theory.
    \item \textbf{Parity with Simple Empirical Quantiles:} Simple non-parametric quantile rules match or exceed the performance of SPOT. On TS-JEPA, taking the validation maximum yields an empirical held-out FPR of $0.0686$ and Point-F1 of $0.1985$, while an empirical 99.5th percentile rule yields an FPR of $0.0838$ and Point-F1 of $0.1995$. Parametric GPD tail modeling provides no demonstrable benefit over simple empirical percentiles on these benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{Failure of Kurtosis-Adaptive Calibration:} The kurtosis-adaptive multiplier rule ($\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau(1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh\gamma_2)$) substantially degrades nominal calibration. On TS-JEPA, it inflates held-out nominal FPR from $0.0651$ to $0.2205$ (with CI excluding $q$ rising from $64\%$ to $84\%$), while yielding a statistically indistinguishable Point-F1 ($0.2113$ vs.\ $0.1968$ under standard SPOT). On Operator-Entropy, it raises nominal FPR from $0.0733$ to $0.1506$. Excess kurtosis measured in training splits does not accurately forecast non-stationary tail shifts on out-of-sample telemetry.
\end{enumerate}

To separate the candidate causes of calibration failure, we recorded per-stream tail-fit diagnostics on a nine-stream non-GHL subset (TS-JEPA and TimesNet, three seeds each; \texttt{reports/evt\_tail\_diagnostics.csv}). Three observations disentangle the mechanism. First, tail-sample scarcity is genuine: the median exceedance count above $u = p_{98}$ is only $21$ (range $16$--$70$), and the Grimshaw estimator returns the degenerate boundary shape $\xi = -0.9$ in $33\%$ of TS-JEPA and $52\%$ of TimesNet fits, so tail-estimation error is real. Second, goodness-of-fit is nonetheless not the dominant failure: a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test of the excesses against each fitted GPD rejects at $p < 0.05$ in only $6/27$ (TS-JEPA) and $8/27$ (TimesNet) cells. Third, realized held-out FPR varies from $0.0$ to $0.67$ across seeds on the \emph{same} stream under fits that pass the KS test --- direct evidence that inter-block distribution drift, not tail mis-specification, drives the overshoot. Exceedances are also strongly clustered (mean of $10$--$22$ exceedances per cluster, more pronounced for TimesNet, whose residuals spike in sustained bursts), which inflates the effective variance of the FPR estimate beyond what an i.i.d.\ tail model assumes. Finally, the empirical 99.5th-percentile rule achieves an equal or lower held-out FPR than SPOT in roughly half the cells ($13/27$ TS-JEPA, $16/27$ TimesNet), confirming that parametric tail extrapolation confers no reliability advantage under this drift regime.

\input{sections/tab_evt_sensitivity.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:evt_sensitivity} provides a multi-parameter sensitivity analysis of the extreme-value calibration procedure across varying initial tail thresholds $u$, theoretical design risk levels $q$, and kurtosis-adaptive coefficients $\beta$. Three key findings emerge:
First, varying the initial threshold from $u=0.95$ to $u=0.99$ produces negligible changes in out-of-sample test FPR ($17.27\%\text{--}17.36\%$) and Point-F1 ($0.1028\text{--}0.1030$), indicating that GPD tail estimates are stable with respect to initial tail percentile selection.
Second, scaling the theoretical design risk $q$ by two orders of magnitude (from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$) shifts validation FPR from $0.0074$ to $0.00009$, but test FPR shifts by less than $1.7\%$ (from $18.95\%$ to $17.27\%$), demonstrating that operational distribution drift completely dominates the theoretical design parameter.
Third, increasing the kurtosis adjustment coefficient $\beta$ systematically degrades performance: at $\beta=0.083$, held-out validation FPR jumps from $0.0019$ to $0.2778$, and at $\beta=0.50$, it collapses to $0.5403$ (test FPR $60.96\%$), flooding the system with alarms. Non-parametric validation rules (such as Max-of-Validation and Empirical 99.5th Percentile) achieve comparable or superior nominal calibration ($0.0000\text{--}0.0054$ validation FPR) without requiring parametric tail assumptions.

\input{sections/tab_evt_failure_decomposition.tex}

To diagnose why extreme-value calibration breaks down on operational telemetry, Table~\ref{tab:evt_failure_decomposition} isolates four statistical mechanisms across benchmark telemetry streams:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Residual Autocorrelation and Extremal Clustering:} The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem \cite{balkema1974residual} requires mutually independent tail exceedances. However, reconstructive observation residuals exhibit serial correlation, with lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho_1 = 0.983 \pm 0.014$ for TimesNet and $\rho_1 = 0.990 \pm 0.007$ for TranAD. Using the Ferro-Segers extremal index estimator \cite{ferro2003inference}, observation exceedances form persistent temporal clusters with mean cluster sizes of $1/\theta = 12.7$ to $13.3$ steps (reaching $45.0$ steps on GECCO water quality telemetry). Clustering collapses the effective independent tail sample size ($N_{\text{eff}} = \theta N_u$) from $N_u = 77\text{--}91$ observations down to $N_{\text{eff}} \approx 2\text{--}6$ independent clusters. In contrast, latent prediction compresses lag-1 autocorrelation to $\rho_1 = 0.820 \pm 0.135$ and mean cluster size to $5.2 \pm 5.8$ steps.
    \item \textbf{Non-Stationary Distribution Drift:} Non-stationarity drives a wedge between calibration and held-out evaluation splits. Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests reveal substantial drift in reconstructive models ($D_{\text{KS}} = 0.357 \pm 0.178$ for TimesNet; $D_{\text{KS}} = 0.388 \pm 0.284$ for TranAD; $p < 10^{-13}$ across streams). This drift shifts the 98th percentile of nominal residuals by $+470.8\%$ on TimesNet and $+48.6\%$ on TranAD between splits. Conversely, TS-JEPA maps temporal sequences into an abstract representation space where environmental shifts are compressed, yielding less than half the KS distance ($D_{\text{KS}} = 0.154 \pm 0.069$) and near-zero relative tail drift ($\Delta q_{98} = -0.36\% \pm 14.1\%$).
    \item \textbf{GPD Goodness-of-Fit Degradation:} Cram{\'e}r-von Mises tests for Generalized Pareto tail convergence reject the stationary GPD null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$) across autocorrelated observation streams. Because parameter estimation fits a stationary tail model to non-stationary, autocorrelated bursts, parametric SPOT thresholds provide no advantage over simple empirical validation percentiles.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Risk Overshoot:} Against the specified design risk $q = 10^{-3}$ ($0.1\%$), TimesNet generates a held-out nominal FPR of $4.32\% \pm 7.60\%$ ($43.2\times$ overshoot), and TranAD produces $4.76\% \pm 10.43\%$ ($47.6\times$ overshoot). By suppressing residual drift and tail clustering, TS-JEPA curbs the empirical nominal FPR to $0.33\% \pm 0.59\%$ ($3.3\times$ overshoot), reducing the discrepancy between theoretical risk and operational deployment by an order of magnitude.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we conducted an empirical investigation into whether Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) and physics-inspired inductive regularizers resolve the false-alarm vulnerability of multivariate time-series anomaly detection. By evaluating 33 continuous telemetry streams across seven physical sensing domains under a strictly held-out calibration protocol, this study yields three primary conclusions:

First, forward prediction in a contracted latent representation space fundamentally conditions residual distributions. By filtering out localized observation-space sensor jitter, latent prediction reduces test residual excess kurtosis and achieves a statistically significant roughly four-fold reduction in out-of-sample nominal false-positive rates compared to reconstructive baselines (paired difference $-0.150$ against TimesNet, $p=0.007$; $-0.150$ against TranAD, $p=0.004$, at five seeds on the multi-seed subset). However, continuous ranking (PR-AUC) and calibrated Point-F1 remain statistically indistinguishable between unconstrained latent prediction and baselines ($p \ge 0.16$). Under strictly causal streaming, latent prediction suppresses false alarms to $1.0\%\text{--}1.9\%$, but at the cost of lower point-level F1 and delayed event recall.

Second, empirical hypothesis testing refutes the claim that physics-inspired regularizers provide an anomaly detection advantage over unconstrained latent prediction. Across all 33 streams, neither Operator-Entropy (spectral rank preservation) nor Reynolds-Stress (second-moment fluctuation alignment) outperforms unconstrained TS-JEPA ($p \ge 0.50$), while Potential-Flow (curl-free gradient dynamics) performs significantly worse ($p=0.013$) due to its inability to represent circulating limit-cycle orbits. In matched-capacity controls holding the neural backbone fixed, disabling each physical loss term changes Point-F1 by $+0.001$ to $+0.006$ ($p \ge 0.58$). While diagnostic experiments confirm that these formulations enforce their intended mathematical properties, real-world telemetry deviations do not obey idealized physical conservation laws, rendering these regularizers ineffective as general anomaly detection priors.

Third, automated threshold calibration via Extreme Value Theory (SPOT) systematically overshoots its theoretical design risk on non-stationary telemetry. At $q=10^{-3}$, empirical nominal false-positive rates settle at $6.5\%\text{--}7.7\%$ for JEPAs and $26.1\%\text{--}26.6\%$ for reconstructive baselines, with 95\% bootstrap intervals excluding $q$ in 64\%--87\% of runs. Parametric EVT tail modeling provides no measurable advantage over simple empirical quantiles, and kurtosis adjustments fail to correct pre-asymptotic tail drift.

\textbf{Scope.} All reported evaluations are \emph{within-dataset}: each model is trained, calibrated, and tested on disjoint chronological partitions of the same telemetry stream. We make no claims about cross-dataset transfer, generalization to previously unseen operating regimes or sensor configurations, or deployment under fault types absent from the training distribution (cf. the injected-fault blind spot quantified in Section~\ref{subsec:gate_fault_study}). Extending these findings to zero-shot or few-shot regime transfer remains open.

Four directions extend this investigation. First, incorporating solenoidal vorticity components into velocity fields via Helmholtz decomposition enables modeling dissipative systems with limit-cycle attractors without sacrificing conservative stability. Second, developing memory-bounded online drift adaptation will allow tracking diurnal thermal cycles and operational setpoint transitions without buffer contamination. Third, implementing accelerated sliding-window inference kernels will enable scaling multi-seed evaluations to million-step telemetry streams. Finally, quantizing JEPA encoders into 8-bit integer formats will support deployment on radiation-hardened satellite microcontrollers and low-power industrial edge hardware.
