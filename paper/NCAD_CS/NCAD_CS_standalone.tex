\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised anomaly detection in multivariate telemetry requires setting operational thresholds automatically via statistical calibration, such as Extreme Value Theory (EVT/SPOT), without ground-truth labels. While deep reconstructive baselines like TimesNet and TranAD achieve competitive ranking (PR-AUC $0.29\text{--}0.34$), heavy-tailed observation residuals cause severe false-alarm flooding (nominal false-positive rates of $24\text{--}30\%$). We investigate whether forecasting in latent representation space via Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) conditions residuals for automated thresholding. Across 33 continuous telemetry streams from seven physical domains evaluated under held-out calibration, latent prediction reduces nominal false-positive rates by 4- to 5-fold to $5.2\text{--}7.9\%$ (paired difference $-0.154$ against TimesNet, $p=0.008$). However, overall Point-F1 ($0.254$ vs. $0.207$, $p=0.55$) and PR-AUC ($0.333$ vs. $0.336$, $p=0.30$) are statistically indistinguishable from baselines, and causal streaming reduces event recall ($10.7\%$ vs. $32.9\%$). Furthermore, matched-capacity controls show that three physics-inspired regularizers (Koopman spectral entropy, Reynolds stress, and potential flow) yield no detectable improvement over unconstrained latent prediction ($p \ge 0.58$). Finally, EVT calibration systematically misses its nominal design risk ($q=10^{-3}$) on non-stationary telemetry, performing similarly to empirical percentiles. Latent prediction thus provides a more conservative detector that suppresses false alarms, but does not improve ranking fidelity or resolve tail calibration drift.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous anomaly detection in multi-sensor telemetry monitors operational health across aerospace systems, Earth observation, cyber-physical infrastructure, and wearable digital healthcare \cite{meng2019spacecraft, chalapathy2019deep}. In remote sensing deployments (such as the NASA Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) satellite, Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) rovers, industrial IoT facilities, and wearable biometric sensor arrays), dozens to hundreds of continuous telemetry channels concurrently track electrical power buses, thermal gradients, mechanical vibrations, and kinematics \cite{Hundman2018b}. When sensor faults or operational breakdowns emerge at the edge, monitoring systems must identify dynamical anomalies before hardware damage occurs \cite{cuellar2024explainable}.

\subsection{Challenges in Modeling Multi-Sensor Telemetry}
From a physical perspective, multi-sensor telemetry reflects the state trajectory of an underlying continuous physical system $\dot{x}(t) = \mathbf{f}(x(t))$. However, attempting to model dynamical equations directly in raw observation space faces four core challenges on edge devices. First, estimating instantaneous time derivatives $\dot{x} \approx \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ directly on discrete, noise-corrupted sensor streams amplifies high-frequency measurement noise, destabilizing numerical trajectory tracking. Second, remote sensing telemetry captures only a noisy surface projection of unmeasured thermodynamic, electrical, and environmental states, violating full-state observability. Third, in large sensor arrays ($K \ge 25$, such as NASA SMAP with $K=25$ and MSL with $K=55$), cross-channel dependencies and non-linear interactions cannot be adequately represented by rigid functional forms in raw signal space. Finally, edge IoT and satellite systems undergo discrete operational mode transitions (e.g., orbital day/night thermal cycling, thruster firings, Parkinsonian gait freezing, cloud server garbage collection) that cause continuous distribution drift.

\subsection{Limitations of Deep Anomaly Detectors}
Deep learning architectures provide data-driven alternatives to explicit equation modeling, yet they face three primary limitations. First, generative models (e.g., autoencoders, VAEs, masked Transformers) minimize point-level $L_1/L_2$ errors in raw signal space \cite{Hundman2018b, xu2022anomaly, wu2023timesnet}. Because reconstruction loss penalizes every discrepancy equally, autoencoders allocate capacity to fitting high-frequency sensor jitter rather than learning nominal dynamical transitions. Second, contrastive frameworks such as Neural Contextual Anomaly Detection (NCAD) \cite{carmona2021neural} train encoders via Contextual Outlier Exposure (COE) using heuristic synthetic perturbations (e.g., Gaussian noise spikes, sequence splicing). However, real physical faults manifest as subtle deviations from nominal dynamics; synthetic injections distort embedding geometry, inducing artificial decision boundaries that generate false alarms on continuous telemetry. Third, in high-dimensional observation space, even after per-channel standardization, Euclidean reconstruction errors ($\|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 = \sum_k (x_k - \hat{x}_k)^2$) accumulate stochastic residual variance across dozens of irrelevant or noisy sensors. Under automated streaming thresholding such as Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT), this residual accumulation yields heavy, unstable tail distributions that distort extreme-value calibrators, triggering false alarm flooding in operational telemetry.

\subsection{Investigating Latent Prediction and Inductive Priors}
To investigate whether operating in latent representation space mitigates observation-space noise drowning without relying on synthetic contrastive perturbations, we study Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) for multivariate telemetry. Rather than reconstructing raw, jitter-corrupted sensor observations $\hat{x} \approx x$, a JEPA maps past observations $x_{\text{ctx}}$ into an abstract representation space and predicts target representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ directly \cite{lecun2022path, assran2023self}. By predicting in latent space, the architecture discards high-frequency stochastic sensor noise that lacks temporal predictability, potentially conditioning prediction residuals for automated tail calibration.

Alongside an unconstrained latent predictor (\texttt{TS-JEPA}), we formalize and test three candidate physics-inspired regularizers hypothesized to stabilize representation dynamics. The first formulation, \textit{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, regularizes the learned latent Koopman transition matrix via spectral entropy to promote high-rank modal expressivity. The second, \textit{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, adapts hydrodynamic Reynolds decomposition ($z = \bar{z} + z'$) to penalize discrepancies in cross-coordinate second-moment fluctuation covariance. The third, \textit{Potential-Flow JEPA}, parameterizes latent transitions as gradients of a scalar potential ($\hat{v} = -\nabla \Phi$), enforcing curl-free dynamics. To isolate channel activity and decouple multi-scale frequencies, these architectures incorporate Causal Coordinate Saliency Gating and Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells, calibrated via Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT).

\subsection{Contributions}
This work makes four principal contributions:

\noindent\textbf{1) Quantifying the Latent Residual Conditioning Effect:} We demonstrate that forecasting telemetry states in abstract representation space rather than reconstructing raw observation waveforms produces residual distributions with lower tail heaviness, lowering measured nominal false-positive rates under SPOT from $24\text{--}30\%$ to $5.2\text{--}7.9\%$ (paired difference $-0.154$ against TimesNet, $p=0.008$; $-0.157$ against TranAD, $p=0.001$). However, Point-F1 and PR-AUC remain statistically indistinguishable from reconstructive baselines, and strictly causal streaming incurs an event recall penalty ($10.7\%$ vs. $32.9\%$), showing that latent prediction provides a conservative, high-precision operating point rather than universal dominance.

\noindent\textbf{2) Rigorous Evaluation of Physics-Inspired Inductive Priors:} We evaluate the three physics-inspired architectures (\texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, \texttt{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, and \texttt{Potential-Flow JEPA}) against unconstrained \texttt{TS-JEPA}. Across matched-capacity controls on fixed neural architectures, switching off the physical regularizers yields no statistically significant difference in Point-F1 ($+0.001$ for entropy, $0.000$ for stress, and $+0.006$ for potential flow; $p \ge 0.58$), documenting rigorous null results for these physical regularizers on real telemetry.

\noindent\textbf{3) Empirical Analysis of Threshold Calibration Failures:} We document that automated EVT/SPOT calibration systematically fails to achieve its theoretical design risk ($q=10^{-3}$) on unlabelled non-stationary telemetry, resulting in empirical false-positive rates of $5\text{--}18\%$ due to distribution drift. We further demonstrate that kurtosis-adaptive heuristics fail to correct this drift, performing comparably to empirical quantile baselines.

\noindent\textbf{4) Open-Source, Reproducible Multi-Seed Benchmark:} We establish a fully reproducible benchmark across 33 matched telemetry streams from seven physical datasets evaluated under a strict held-out calibration protocol (chronological 65/35 train/held-out split with a 64-step gap, up to three seeds, paired bootstrap confidence intervals, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests). All code, checkpoints, and evaluation logs are released open-source.

Section~\ref{sec:dataset} details the multi-sensor telemetry benchmark suite and the held-out partition protocol. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_method} formalizes the JEPA formulations, causal saliency gating, dyadic wavelets, and EVT calibration. Section~\ref{sec:exp} establishes the experimental setup and evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} evaluates architectural components and inductive regularizer controls. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents quantitative benchmark results, threshold calibration analyses, and computational profiles. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Unsupervised time-series anomaly detection spans dynamical equation discovery, reconstructive generative modeling, contrastive representation learning, and predictive world models. This section reviews these approaches and positions our Physics-Inspired JEPA framework.

\subsection{Dynamical Systems Modeling and Koopman Operators}
The physical modeling of multi-sensor telemetry has long sought to capture system behavior as continuous dynamical trajectories governed by differential equations $\dot{x}(t) = \mathbf{f}(x(t))$ \cite{brunton2016discovering}. One prominent approach, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) \cite{chen2018neural}, parameterizes continuous velocity fields using neural networks $\frac{dx}{dt} = f_\theta(x(t), t)$ and integrates trajectories numerically. While Neural ODEs learn flexible vector fields, fitting them directly in raw observation space forces numerical solvers to integrate through high-frequency stochastic sensor noise, leading to numerical stiffness, slow training, and sensitivity to measurement jitter.

In parallel, Koopman operator theory and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) \cite{brunton2016koopman, lusch2018deep, liu2023koopa} analyze non-linear dynamical systems by learning linear transition operators that act on observable functions. Modern deep Koopman frameworks (e.g., Koopa \cite{liu2023koopa}) learn observable embeddings for non-stationary forecasting. In anomaly detection, physical disruptions manifest as spectral decoherence or sudden changes in modal dissipation. Rather than operating on uncalibrated raw signals, our framework parameterizes latent linear transitions regularized by Koopman spectral entropy during training directly in representation space.

\subsection{Reconstruction-Based Detection}
The predominant deep learning approach in time-series anomaly detection relies on learning nominal patterns through raw signal reconstruction. Hundman et al. \cite{Hundman2018b} introduced LSTM-based prediction coupled with non-parametric dynamic thresholding for NASA spacecraft telemetry (SMAP/MSL). Modern architectures incorporate specialized attention and frequency-domain transformations. For example, Anomaly Transformer \cite{xu2022anomaly} introduces Anomaly-Attention to quantify the association discrepancy between local prior Gaussian kernel associations and global series self-attention. TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet} decomposes 1D multivariate time series into 2D temporal variations using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) periodicity discovery and 2D Inception blocks. Similarly, TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad} employs a two-phase adversarial Transformer with sub-series attention and focus scoring to amplify subtle reconstruction errors.

Despite their empirical popularity, reconstructive methods share a fundamental vulnerability: signal-level noise memorization. Because loss functions optimize point-level $L_1$ or $L_2$ differences in raw signal space, reconstructive networks are biased toward fitting high-frequency stochastic noise rather than learning invariant dynamical laws. In addition, during sustained anomalies, corrupted observations contaminate the context window, causing the reconstructor to adapt and reconstruct the anomaly as the nominal state.

\subsection{Forecasting-Based Detection and Threshold Calibration}
An alternative to reconstruction is temporal forecasting, which models the conditional distribution of future observations given past context. Classical autoregressive models and deep probabilistic forecasters (such as DeepAR \cite{salinas2020deepar}) predict future values and flag observations whose prediction error exceeds normal bounds. While forecasting prevents full-sequence autoencoders from memorizing anomalous segments through bidirectional receptive fields, forecasting in raw observation space remains vulnerable to the same noise accumulation problem: in high-dimensional sensor arrays, point-level Euclidean forecasting errors accumulate variance across dozens of irrelevant channels.

Following score generation, unsupervised anomaly detectors require an automated calibration rule to convert continuous residual scores into binary operational alarms without access to test labels. Hundman et al. \cite{Hundman2018b} introduced non-parametric dynamic thresholding via sliding window error distributions. More recently, extreme value theory, notably Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}, has become standard for automated thresholding, fitting Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) tails to residual exceedances under a specified risk parameter $q$. However, asymptotic EVT guarantees rely on independent and identically distributed tail observations. On real-world multi-sensor telemetry, temporal autocorrelation, non-stationary operational drift, and heavy tails can cause extreme-value calibrators to deviate substantially from their nominal design risk.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning and Representation-Based Detection}
To avoid reconstructing raw noise, contrastive methods learn discriminative representations by separating nominal sequences from anomalous perturbations. Neural Contextual Anomaly Detection (NCAD) \cite{carmona2021neural} uses Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) trained with Contextual Outlier Exposure (COE), injecting artificial synthetic anomalies (Gaussian noise, point spikes, trend shifts) into training windows to create negative pairs. However, synthetic injections distort the physical dynamical manifold and fail to generalize to subtle real-world physical faults (such as gradual thermal dissipation or Parkinsonian gait freezing). Alternatively, DCdetector \cite{yang2023dcdetector} replaces reconstruction with multi-scale dual-attention contrastive representation learning, maximizing cross-scale representation consistency. While DCdetector eliminates artificial injection heuristics, contrastive objectives require careful scale selection and representation pooling.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Time-Series Representation Learning and JEPAs}
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA), originally proposed by LeCun \cite{lecun2022path} and demonstrated in computer vision by I-JEPA \cite{assran2023self} and V-JEPA \cite{bardes2024vjepa}, predict target representations from context in latent space. Non-contrastive regularizers such as Barlow Twins \cite{zbontar2021barlow}, W-MSE \cite{ermolov2021whitening}, and VICReg \cite{bardes2021vicreg} prevent representation collapse without negative mining.

Recent works have extended self-supervised and predictive representation learning principles to time-series sequences. TS2Vec \cite{yue2022ts2vec} learns multi-scale representations through contextual and temporal contrasting, while Eldele et al. \cite{eldele2021time} enforce temporal-contextual cross-view consistency. Systematic surveys by Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2023self} document the emergence of self-supervised representation architectures across multi-sensor streams. However, existing time-series representation frameworks lack explicit physical dynamical priors, leaving latent trajectories unanchored to physical conservation and spectral dissipation principles.

\subsection{Physics-Inspired Deep Learning and Structured Inductive Biases}
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) \cite{raissi2019physics}, alongside Hamiltonian \cite{greydanus2019hamiltonian} and Lagrangian Neural Networks \cite{cranmer2020lagrangian}, integrate dynamical principles into neural architectures. However, while Hamiltonian networks enforce strict energy conservation for closed mechanical systems, standard PINNs face two primary limitations in high-dimensional sensor telemetry. First, jointly optimizing data fidelity and PDE residual loss leads to stiff, competing gradient dynamics, often causing training instability or failure to converge in high noise regimes \cite{wang2021understanding}. Second, complex IoT and aerospace telemetry (e.g., SMAP, MSL, and cyber-physical water networks) rarely follow a single closed-form PDE whose analytical derivatives can be evaluated across dozens of uncalibrated sensors. Rather than enforcing analytical differential equations through auxiliary training penalties, our framework incorporates physical conservation, turbulent transport, and spectral dissipation principles directly into latent representations as inductive regularizers, avoiding multi-loss balancing conflicts.

\subsection{Clarification of Technical Terminology}
To prevent ambiguity, we establish precise definitions for recurring technical terms used throughout this manuscript:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physics-Inspired vs. Physics-Informed:} Physics-informed methods enforce known governing equations (such as Navier-Stokes or wave equations) directly via loss residuals. In contrast, physics-inspired methods adopt mathematical structures suggested by physical theories (such as spectral entropy or second-moment closures) as exploratory inductive regularizers on learned representations, without claiming that the latent coordinates correspond to physical state variables.
    \item \textbf{Causal Preprocessing vs. Causal Decision-Time Access:} Causal preprocessing indicates that feature transformations (such as coordinate saliency gating) are computed strictly from historical context observations ($\partial \mathbf{g}/\partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$). Causal decision-time access specifies whether operational alarms are issued with zero lookahead latency ($0$-lookahead) or after collecting a buffered prospective window ($S$-lookahead).
    \item \textbf{Conservative vs. Dissipative:} Conservative dynamics preserve total energy and have zero curl ($\nabla \times v = 0$). Autonomous gradient flows $\dot{z} = -\nabla\Phi$ are strictly dissipative with respect to the potential $\Phi$, satisfying $\frac{d}{d\tau}\Phi \le 0$ and precluding periodic orbits.
    \item \textbf{Operator Non-Degeneracy vs. Representation Anti-Collapse:} Operator non-degeneracy ensures that the learned transition operator $\mathbf{K}$ does not collapse to zero or rank 1. Representation anti-collapse refers to the separate property that the encoder does not map distinct time-series inputs to a constant latent vector.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Positioning of the Proposed Framework}
This investigation bridges joint-embedding predictive learning and multivariate time-series anomaly detection through four structural elements. First, by forecasting future representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ rather than reconstructing raw observations $\hat{x}$, our models avoid high-frequency noise drowning that affects reconstructive baselines (TimesNet, TranAD). Second, we formulate three candidate physics-inspired regularizers: Operator-Entropy (spectral rank preservation), Reynolds-Stress (second-moment fluctuation alignment), and Potential-Flow (curl-free gradient dynamics), evaluating them empirically against unconstrained latent controls. Third, we incorporate Causal Coordinate Saliency Gating to isolate active sensors without target leakage, and Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelets to separate transient impulses from low-frequency operational drift. Finally, we evaluate Extreme Value Theory (EVT / SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly} on strictly held-out nominal telemetry splits to quantify the gap between theoretical design risk and empirical false-alarm rates.

\section{Multi-Sensor IoT and Remote Sensing Benchmark Suite}
\label{sec:dataset}

To evaluate Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures and candidate physical regularizers across diverse edge, cyber-physical, and orbital sensing dynamics, our benchmark suite comprises 11 datasets spanning 45 continuous telemetry streams sourced from NASA spacecraft archives \cite{Hundman2018b}, the UCI Machine Learning Repository \cite{bachlin2010wearable, asuncion2007uci}, TimeEval \cite{schmidl2022anomaly}, and mTSBench \cite{zhou2026mtsbench}. We evaluate 33 streams under our primary multi-seed held-out calibration protocol and reserve 12 streams for extended operational case studies. We organize these streams into four physical sensing domains:

\subsection{Domain 1: Satellite and Spacecraft Telemetry (13 Streams)}
This domain covers high-reliability orbital and deep-space telemetry. The \textit{NASA SMAP Earth Observation Satellite} \cite{Hundman2018b} (6 evaluated streams: \texttt{A-1}, \texttt{A-2}, \texttt{A-7}, \texttt{D-3}, \texttt{P-3}, \texttt{E-1}) provides continuous telemetry values alongside one-hot command vectors monitoring power, thermal, and attitude control subsystems. The \textit{NASA MSL Curiosity Rover} \cite{Hundman2018b} (6 evaluated streams: \texttt{C-1}, \texttt{C-2}, \texttt{D-14}, \texttt{D-15}, \texttt{D-16}, \texttt{M-1}) tracks robotic actuator, joint drive, and environmental streams during planetary surface operations. Finally, the \textit{SWAN-SF Space Weather Telemetry} \cite{angryk2020multivariate} (1 stream) provides solar photospheric vector magnetogram telemetry from NASA SDO/HMI, tracking active region magnetic field perturbations during solar eruptive events.

\subsection{Domain 2: Wearable Biometrics and Kinematics (6 Streams)}
This domain evaluates human kinematic telemetry via the \textit{Daphnet Wearable Body Sensor Network} \cite{bachlin2010wearable} (6 evaluated streams: \texttt{S01R01E1} to \texttt{S03R02E0}). Telemetry consists of 64~Hz tri-axial accelerometry recorded from wearable sensor nodes placed on the shank, thigh, and trunk to track Freezing-of-Gait (FoG) motor breakdown events in Parkinson's disease patients. At 64~Hz, the suspect horizon $S = 64$ corresponds to a 1.0-second lookahead window.

\subsection{Domain 3: Industrial Processes and Cyber-Physical Systems (16 Streams)}
This domain covers continuous industrial processes and fluid transportation. The \textit{GHL Gasoil Heating Loop} (14 benchmark streams) captures thermal and fluid process loop telemetry across temperature, flow rate, and valve pressure dynamics. The \textit{Genesis Automation Testbed} (1 stream) tracks manufacturing automation telemetry from an industrial pick-and-place demonstrator during mechanical handling faults. The \textit{GECCO Water Distribution IoT} (1 stream) monitors physical water distribution infrastructure through continuous chemical and hydrological measurements at 1-minute resolution (where $S=64$ represents a 64-minute operational horizon).

\subsection{Domain 4: Smart Infrastructure and Environmental Sensing (10 Streams)}
This domain evaluates urban infrastructure and cyber-physical networks. The \textit{Room-Occupancy Environmental IoT} dataset \cite{candanedo2016accurate} (2 streams) records ambient environmental conditions ($\text{CO}_2$, light, temperature, humidity) tracking room-occupancy state switches. The \textit{CICIDS Edge Network Telemetry} (6 streams) captures network flow dynamics, tracking gateway protocol shifts and security anomalies including DDoS bursts and port scans. The \textit{CalIt2 Building People-Flow Telemetry} \cite{asuncion2007uci} (1 stream) records continuous pedestrian counts via optical beam sensors at the UC Irvine Calit2 facility entrance. Finally, the \textit{Metro Highway Traffic} dataset (1 stream) tracks interstate highway traffic volume on Minnesota Interstate I-94, where anomalies correspond to major traffic disruption and incident events.

Each benchmark stream provides nominal training sequences and testing sequences containing labeled ground-truth anomalies. Continuous telemetry channels are standardized via training statistics ($\bm{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \bm{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$), flooring standard deviations at $\max(\bm{\sigma}, 10^{-2})$ to prevent division by zero during flatline operational phases. While mission archives contain larger entity counts (the complete SMAP archive includes 55 telemetry entities and MSL includes 27 entities across subsystems), our benchmark evaluates standardized subsets studied in the telemetry anomaly detection literature \cite{Hundman2018b, schmidl2022anomaly} to ensure strict matching and reproducible runtime conditions across all baselines.

To eliminate circular in-sample calibration and measure out-of-sample false-alarm rates under automated thresholding, we enforce a strict held-out calibration protocol. For each stream, the nominal training sequence is partitioned chronologically: the first 65\% is used to train model representations; the remaining 35\% is reserved for held-out evaluation, split into a calibration half (used to fit extreme-value thresholds without seeing test anomalies) and an evaluation half (separated by a 64-step isolation gap) on which nominal false-positive rates (FPR) are measured on data the threshold never saw. This multi-seed held-out protocol completes across 33 streams spanning seven distinct physical datasets: 6 Daphnet (wearable kinematics), 14 GHL (fluid transport), 4 MSL (planetary rovers), 6 SMAP (Earth observation satellites), Genesis (manufacturing automation), Metro (highway traffic), and SWAN (solar space weather). Streams with nominal blocks too short to support a calibration and an evaluation half with an isolation gap (CalIt2, Room-Occupancy, and two MSL streams) or where multi-seed stride-1 bootstrap evaluation over $10^6$ steps was computationally prohibitive (GECCO, CICIDS) are documented in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_specs}.

Table~\ref{tab:dataset_specs} provides the technical specifications across the benchmark suite, totaling $2{,}079{,}507$ nominal training timesteps and $2{,}986{,}574$ test timesteps (over 5.06 million observations). We explicitly document the physical domain, channel dimension $K$, sampling resolution, sequence lengths, anomaly contamination ratio $\pi\%$, anomaly failure modes, and partition protocols to ensure experimental reproducibility.

\label{sec:proposed_method}

This section formalizes the mathematical formulation and inductive biases of the proposed framework. 

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Latent Space Prediction}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$ denote a continuous multivariate time-series sequence consisting of $K$ observed sensor variables over $T$ timesteps. The sequence is partitioned into historical context $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ and suspect/target horizon $x_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$, where $C$ and $S$ denote the respective temporal horizons.

Existing deep learning baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer) frame anomaly detection as pointwise reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
    \min_\theta \mathbb{E}\left[ \| \mathbf{X} - \mathcal{D}_\theta(\mathcal{E}_\theta(\mathbf{X})) \|_F^2 \right].
\end{equation}
In complex multi-sensor telemetry, ambient high-frequency stochastic jitter dominates the Frobenius norm, causing models to waste representational bandwidth reconstructing sensor noise rather than modeling system state dynamics (leading to noise drowning).

To overcome this, we adopt the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) framework \cite{lecun2022path}. A context encoder $E_\theta$ maps $x_{\text{ctx}} \to z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, while an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) momentum target encoder $E_\phi$ maps $x_{\text{tgt}} \to z_{\text{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, updated via:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k \leftarrow m_k \phi_{k-1} + (1 - m_k) \theta_k, \quad m_k \in [0.996, 0.9995].
\end{equation}
The core objective is to predict target representations in latent space under physical dynamical constraints.

\subsection{The Physics-Inspired JEPA Triad}
\label{subsec:triad_formulations}
Rather than relying on unconstrained Euclidean regression, we incorporate physical domain regularizers through three complementary geometric formulations:

\subsubsection{Operator-Entropy JEPA (Spectral Dynamics \& Modal Dispersion)}
In dynamical systems theory, non-linear trajectories can be analyzed via linear operators acting on observable functions (Koopman operator theory \cite{koopman1931hamiltonian, mezic2005spectral}). In our architecture, the encoder maps the observation window to a finite-dimensional latent observable vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^D$. We parameterize the predictive transition between context and target latent states via a learned transition operator $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}.
\end{equation}
To prevent the operator from collapsing onto a degenerate low-rank subspace (dimensional collapse) or degenerating to zero (energy collapse), we formulate an operator-entropy regularizer that decouples spectral uniformity from total operator energy.

Let $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_D \ge 0$ denote the singular values of $\mathbf{K}$, with corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2$ of the symmetric Gram matrix $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T$. The total operator Frobenius energy is $\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}) = \|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^D \lambda_i$. For any non-zero operator ($\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{K}) > 0$), we define the scale-free spectral density distribution $\mathbf{p} = [p_1, \dots, p_D]^T$ over latent modes:
\begin{equation}
    p_i = \frac{\lambda_i(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T)}{\sum_{j=1}^D \lambda_j(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^T)} = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2}, \quad \sum_{i=1}^D p_i = 1, \quad p_i \ge 0.
\end{equation}
We omit isotropic diagonal loading ($\frac{\epsilon}{D}\mathbf{I}$) from the Gram matrix before normalization; adding it would cause $\mathbf{K} \to \mathbf{0}$ to yield an artificial identity spectrum with maximal entropy, spuriously rewarding total collapse. Instead, we evaluate the Von Neumann spectral entropy directly on the scale-free modal probabilities with the standard analytic convention $0 \log 0 = 0$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = -\sum_{i: p_i > 0} p_i \log p_i.
\end{equation}
The \textit{Spectral Entropy Deficiency} quantifies the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the uniform modal equipartition $\mathbf{u} = [1/D, \dots, 1/D]^T$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K}) = \log D - \mathcal{H}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\mathbf{p} \,\|\, \mathbf{u}) \ge 0.
\end{equation}
Here, $\Delta\mathcal{H} = 0$ if and only if all modes carry equal energy ($\sigma_1 = \dots = \sigma_D$), while dimensional collapse to rank 1 yields maximal deficiency $\Delta\mathcal{H} = \log D$.

To penalize energy collapse ($\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0$) and prevent explosive gain, we introduce a non-negative Bregman energy barrier relative to nominal isometric scale $\mathcal{E}_0 = \gamma D$ ($\gamma = 1.0$):
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{K}) = r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) - 1 - \log r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) \ge 0, \quad r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{K}) = \max\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F^2}{\gamma D}, \epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ provides a numerical floor. For the identity operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{I}$ with $\gamma=1.0$, $\|\mathbf{I}\|_F^2 = D$, yielding $r_{\text{safe}}(\mathbf{I}) = 1.0 > \epsilon$ and $\Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{I}) = 1 - 1 - \log 1 = 0$ exactly. The barrier diverges to $+\infty$ as $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0$. The composite spectral regularizer is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) = \Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{K}) + \beta_{\text{norm}} \Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{K}),
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{\text{norm}} = 1.0$. The total training objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Op-Entropy}} = \| \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}} - z_{\text{tgt}} \|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{ent}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{ent}} = 0.5$ (the value used in all experiments). The implementation also applies a radial-heat damping layer, a Cohn--Elkies shell filter and a resolvent purification step to the latent vectors before the loss; these are shared with the $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$ control in Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations}. We emphasize that $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ acts directly on the transition operator matrix $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$, enforcing a non-collapsed, full-rank operator spectrum; it does not provide an analytical guarantee against representation collapse in the encoder itself. In practice, representation collapse in the encoder is mitigated through the asymmetric Exponential Moving Average (EMA) target encoder dynamics and stop-gradient operations common to self-supervised frameworks (e.g., BYOL \cite{grill2020bootstrap}, I-JEPA \cite{assran2023self}), rather than by $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ directly.

\begin{proposition}[Operator Triviality Invariance]
\label{prop:anti_collapse}
Let $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \|\mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)} - z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)}\|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{ent}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K})$ with $\lambda_{\text{ent}} > 0, \beta_{\text{norm}} > 0$, and non-trivial target batch $\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \|z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)}\|_2^2 = E_{\text{tgt}} > 0$. The zero operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ is strictly not a local or global minimizer of $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K})$, and $\lim_{\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) = +\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$, prediction error is finite ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}(\mathbf{0}) = E_{\text{tgt}} < \infty$). Since $\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0$, $\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}(\mathbf{K}) \ge \beta_{\text{norm}}(r_{\text{safe}} - 1 - \log r_{\text{safe}})$. As $\|\mathbf{K}\|_F \to 0$, $r_{\text{safe}} \to \epsilon$ and $-\log r_{\text{safe}} \to -\log \epsilon$, and in the unfloored continuum limit $r \to 0^+$, $-\log r \to +\infty$, driving $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{K}) \to +\infty$. Conversely, the identity operator $\mathbf{K}_0 = \mathbf{I}$ satisfies $\Delta\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{I}) = 0$ and $\Phi_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{I}) = 0$, yielding strictly finite loss $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{I}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \|z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)} - z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)}\|_2^2 < \infty$. Hence $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{0}$ cannot be a minimizer.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Remark (Operator Non-Degeneracy vs. Representation Collapse):} Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse} establishes that the transition operator $\mathbf{K}$ cannot degenerate to $\mathbf{0}$ or collapse to rank 1 when operating on non-trivial target representations. However, it does not prevent encoder representation collapse. If the encoder $E_\theta$ maps all inputs to a constant representation $z_{\text{ctx}}^{(b)} = z_{\text{tgt}}^{(b)} = \mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, the operator $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{I}$ achieves zero prediction error and zero spectral penalty while carrying zero information about the input time series. Prevention of constant representation collapse depends empirically on the stop-gradient momentum encoder dynamics rather than Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse}.

\subsubsection{Reynolds-Stress JEPA (Second-Moment Closure Head)}
\label{subsubsec:reynolds_stress_jepa}
Reynolds decomposition splits a turbulent velocity field into a mean flow and a fluctuation component, where the Reynolds stress tensor captures the second moment of turbulent velocity fluctuations. We adapt the second-moment formulation as an inductive bias in latent representation space. The latent coordinates are learned feature embeddings rather than physical velocity components, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is not a physical momentum-transport tensor.

The context encoder output is passed through a Hankel moment filter and a shearing-wavelet pulse block. A two-layer MLP predicts the target latent, $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}$, followed by a Cohn--Elkies shell filter. The target encoder (EMA, stop-gradient) produces $z_{\text{tgt}}$ through the same filters. A learned linear map $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s \times D}$ ($d_s = 16$) yields the empirical second-moment matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}} = (\mathbf{P} z_{\text{tgt}})(\mathbf{P} z_{\text{tgt}})^T + 10^{-3}\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s \times d_s}.
\end{equation}
Because $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}}$ is computed from an instantaneous latent vector rather than averaged over time across the target horizon $S$, it forms a rank-one outer product per sample rather than a true temporal sample covariance. Statistically, it imposes an auxiliary second-moment projection penalty. A closure head $h_\psi$ with an admissible-cone projection predicts $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}} = h_\psi(z_{\text{ctx}})$ from the context latent. The training objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Reynolds}} = \operatorname{MSE}(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}, z_{\text{tgt}}) + 0.1\, \operatorname{MSE}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}}).
\end{equation}
At inference the anomaly score is $\mathcal{S}(t) = g(z_{\text{tgt}} - \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}) + 0.5\, g(\operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}}))$, where $g$ is the latent coordinate-saliency gate. The stress term enters both the training loss and the test score. In Section~\ref{subsec:eval_physics_priors} and Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a), controlled ablations that disable the stress loss and score terms show that this auxiliary second-moment objective yields no statistically detectable improvement over standard latent regression ($p \ge 0.375$).

\subsubsection{Potential-Flow JEPA (Conservative Field Theory)}
\label{subsubsec:potential_flow_jepa}
Under the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition theorem, any sufficiently smooth vector field $v \in C^1(\mathbb{R}^D, \mathbb{R}^D)$ can be resolved into an irrotational (curl-free) conservative potential gradient and a solenoidal (divergence-free) rotational field:
\begin{equation}
    v = -\nabla \Phi + \nabla \times A.
\end{equation}
In nominal physical equilibria, dissipative dynamical systems relax toward local minima on potential energy surfaces. Continuous latent trajectory evolution can be modeled as a dissipative gradient flow:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{z}(\tau) = -\nabla_z \Phi_\psi(z(\tau)),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_\psi: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar potential network parameterized with smooth $C^2$ activation functions (e.g., SiLU). Along continuous trajectories of this autonomous system, the potential satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau} \Phi_\psi(z(\tau)) = \langle \nabla_z \Phi_\psi(z), \dot{z}(\tau) \rangle = -\|\nabla_z \Phi_\psi(z(\tau))\|_2^2 \le 0,
\end{equation}
rendering $\Phi_\psi$ a strict Lyapunov function. Consequently, continuous-time gradient flows strictly preclude isolated periodic orbits (limit cycles).

\paragraph{Continuous Velocity Field and Harmonic Grassmannian Regimes}
Rather than parameterizing a discrete unconstrained displacement, our implementation constructs the continuous velocity field as the negative gradient of a scalar potential field $\Phi_\psi$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{v}(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) = -\nabla_{z_t} \Phi_\psi(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_\psi$ is a three-layer residual MLP with LayerNorm and SiLU activations. The network is conditioned on the current state $z_t$, the continuous diffusion time $t \in [0, 1]$ via sinusoidal embeddings, the context representation $z_{\text{ctx}}$, and a discrete regime projection $p_{\text{regime}}$. The raw Euclidean gradient is projected onto the tangent bundle $T_{z_t} \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ via \texttt{MovingTangentProjection}, stabilizing discrete trajectory integration and bounding the effective Lipschitz curvature.

Discrete operational modes are modeled via a Harmonic Grassmannian Codebook maintaining $K=4$ orthonormal subspace frames $\mathbf{U}_k \in \operatorname{Gr}(d, D)$ ($d=8, D=32$, satisfying $\mathbf{U}_k^\top \mathbf{U}_k = \mathbf{I}_d$). The active regime projection $p_{\text{regime}}$ is selected via maximum subspace energy capture:
\begin{equation}
    k^* = \arg\max_{k \in \{1,\dots,K\}} \|\mathbf{U}_k^\top z_{\text{ctx}}\|_2^2, \quad p_{\text{regime}} = \mathbf{U}_{k^*} \mathbf{U}_{k^*}^\top z_{\text{ctx}}.
\end{equation}
During training, the frames are regularized with a Delsarte frame separation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{grass}} = \frac{2}{K(K-1)} \sum_{i < j} \|\mathbf{U}_i^\top \mathbf{U}_j\|_F^2$. In Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations}, a no-regime control ($K=1$, $p_{\text{regime}} = \mathbf{0}$) evaluates performance when regime routing is disabled.

\paragraph{Flow-Matching Objective, Numerical Midpoint Evaluation, and Dynamical Misspecification}
We explicitly distinguish the continuous dynamical system from the empirical learning and inference objective. The theoretical Lyapunov dissipation argument applies to autonomous continuous gradient descent $\dot{z} = -\nabla\Phi(z)$. In contrast, our architecture trains $\hat{v} = -\nabla_{z_t}\Phi_\psi(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}})$ via conditional flow matching (OT-CFM) along linear interpolation paths $z_t = (1-t)z_{\text{noise}} + t z_{\text{tgt}}$, where $t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$ and $z_{\text{noise}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{Pot-Flow}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, z_{\text{noise}}} \left[ \| \hat{v}(z_t, t, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) - (z_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{noise}}) \|_2^2 \right].
\end{equation}
At inference time, rather than numerically integrating an autonomous ODE to equilibrium, the detector performs a single-step midpoint evaluation at trajectory time $t=0.5$ with $z_{\text{eval}} = 0.5 z_{\text{tgt}}$ against the target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$: $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{v}(z_{\text{eval}}, 0.5, z_{\text{ctx}}, p_{\text{regime}}) - z_{\text{tgt}}$. The anomaly score is computed by passing $\mathbf{e}(t)$ through the latent coordinate-saliency gate: $\mathcal{S}(t) = g(\mathbf{e}(t))$.

Consequently, parameterizing $\hat{v} = -\nabla\Phi$ guarantees that the predicted velocity field is irrotational ($\nabla \times \hat{v} = \mathbf{0}$) at each instant of diffusion time $t$, but does not imply that the observed physical telemetry is conservative or governed by a potential function. In real industrial and biological telemetry characterized by periodic oscillations or limit cycles (such as gait rhythms in Daphnet), this irrotational restriction causes severe model misspecification, explaining why Potential-Flow JEPA underperforms unconstrained TS-JEPA ($F_1$ difference $-0.091, p=0.020$).

\subsection{Anomaly Scoring and Operational Detection Latency}
\label{subsec:scoring_function}
During inference, sliding context windows $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :]$ and suspect target windows $x_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :]$ are processed with a stride of 1 timestep. For TS-JEPA, Operator-Entropy JEPA, and Potential-Flow JEPA, the anomaly score is computed by applying the latent coordinate-saliency gate $g(\cdot)$ (Section~\ref{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}) to the latent discrepancy vector $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}(t) - z_{\text{tgt}}(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}(t) = g(\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}(t) - z_{\text{tgt}}(t)).
\end{equation}
For Reynolds-Stress JEPA, the score incorporates both the gated mean discrepancy and the gated fluctuation tensor residual: $\mathcal{S}(t) = g(z_{\text{tgt}} - \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}) + 0.5\, g(\operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{obs}} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{pred}}))$. Context and target representations across all JEPA variants pass through shared filtering stages: Hankel moment filters, Littlewood--Paley dyadic frequency shells, Cohn--Elkies shell filters, and resolvent purification layers. These modules are held fixed across the ablation controls in Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations} to isolate the physical regularizers.

\noindent\textbf{Operational Distinction: Zero-Lookahead Causal Filtering vs. Buffered Predictive Early Warning.}
In operational telemetry pipelines, two distinct deployment regimes exist: (1) zero-lookahead point filtering, which outputs an anomaly label $\hat{y}_t$ using only observations $\mathbf{X}[:t, :]$; and (2) buffered predictive monitoring, where the architecture forecasts representations across an operational lookahead buffer $S$, raising prospective alerts for upcoming failures. TS-JEPA operates under regime (2): given historical context $x_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t-C:t, :]$, it projects future latent trajectory $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}}$ over $[t, t+S]$. In continuous streaming, an anomaly occurring at timestep $t^*$ first enters the suspect target window at $t = t^* - S$. Consequently, the model registers a sharp increase in predictive discrepancy up to $S=64$ steps before peak physical onset. In our benchmark, we enforce strict unadjusted point-level evaluation at each decision time $t$ ($\text{stride}=1$). Because ground-truth point labels are strictly aligned to the physical anomaly window $[t^*, t^* + L]$, early warnings generated during the pre-onset interval $[t^* - S, t^*]$ are formally scored as point-level false alarms ($FP$). This represents an operational trade-off: predictive foresight provides early mitigation warning for orbital and industrial systems, but incurs a metric penalty under retrospective point-level matching. In operational incident analysis (Section VII.D), we also evaluate \textit{Middle} alignment, where the anomaly score is centered at $t + S/2$ to balance pre-onset and post-onset window overlap. Baseline models (TimesNet, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer, DCdetector, and NCAD) are evaluated under the exact identical sliding-window evaluation protocol, lookahead horizon, and standardization.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Streaming Anomaly Scoring and Decision Calibration}
\label{alg:scoring_protocol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Streaming telemetry $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}$, context horizon $C=256$, suspect horizon $S=64$, deployment mode $\mathcal{M} \in \{\text{Causal}, \text{Buffered}\}$, calibrator $\mathcal{C}_{\text{EVT}}$ fit to nominal residuals.
\Ensure Point-level alarm stream $\hat{y}_t \in \{0, 1\}$ for $t \in [C, T]$.
\State Initialize score buffer $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^T \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$, hit counts $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^T \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$.
\For{$t = C$ \textbf{to} $T - S$}
    \State Extract historical context: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[t - C : t, :]$
    \State Apply causal saliency gating: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{ctx}} \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} \odot \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ \Comment{Zero target leakage}
    \State Encode latent context: $z_{\text{ctx}} \leftarrow E_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{ctx}})$
    \State Forecast future latent representation: $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow P_\psi(z_{\text{ctx}})$
    \State Extract prospective suspect horizon: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow \mathbf{X}[t : t + S, :]$
    \State Encode target representation: $z_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow E_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \odot \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}}))$
    \State Compute latent predictive discrepancy: $\mathcal{D}_t \leftarrow \|\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}}\|_2^2$
    \If{$\mathcal{M} = \text{Causal}$ \textbf{($0$-Lookahead Delay)}}
        \State Assign score strictly to current observed step: $s_t \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_t$
        \State $\hat{y}_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(s_t > \tau_{\text{eff}})$ \Comment{Zero future delay}
    \ElsIf{$\mathcal{M} = \text{Buffered}$ \textbf{($S$-Lookahead Delay)}}
        \For{$\tau = t$ \textbf{to} $t + S - 1$}
            \State Accumulate suspect overlap: $s_\tau \leftarrow s_\tau + \mathcal{D}_t$, \quad $c_\tau \leftarrow c_\tau + 1$
        \EndFor
        \State At lookahead finalization step $t$: $\bar{s}_t \leftarrow s_t / \max(c_t, 1)$
        \State $\hat{y}_t \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(\bar{s}_t > \tau_{\text{eff}})$ \Comment{Buffered running average}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return Binary alarm sequence $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Shared Internal Inductive Biases}
\label{subsec:shared_inductive_biases}
To stabilize learning across high-dimensional, noisy telemetry, all three architectures incorporate three inductive mechanisms:

\subsubsection{Causal Coordinate Saliency Gating and Zero-Leakage Shielding}
\label{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}
On high-dimensional multivariate streams ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults frequently originate within a localized subsystem (e.g., a single thruster, thermal sensor, or battery cell), while the remaining $K-1$ channels exhibit nominal ambient noise. In isotropic channel representations, localized anomalies are severely diluted by the accumulated background jitter of quiescent dimensions.

To prevent dimensional drowning without introducing target-window leakage, we formulate a strictly causal \textit{Causal Coordinate Saliency Gate}. Given historical context $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}} = \mathbf{X}[t-C:t, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ and prospective target horizon $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{X}[t:t+S, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$, the channel dispersion metrics are computed \textit{strictly and exclusively} from past context observations:
\begin{equation}
    v_k^{\text{ctx}} = \operatorname{Var}_{\tau \in [t-C, t]}(x_{\tau, k}) = \frac{1}{C-1} \sum_{\tau=t-C}^t \left(x_{\tau, k} - \bar{x}_k^{\text{ctx}}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_k = \frac{v_k^{\text{ctx}}}{\sum_{j=1}^K v_j^{\text{ctx}} + \epsilon}, \quad g_k = \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid, $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ prevents division by zero, $\bar{s} = 1/K$ represents the uniform variance share expectation across all $K$ channels, and $\sigma_s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (s_k - \bar{s})^2 + \epsilon}$ denotes the cross-channel dispersion of variance shares. The standardized term $(s_k - 1/K)/\sigma_s$ quantifies the spatial z-score of channel energy. 

The resulting causal modulation vector $\mathbf{g} = [g_1, \dots, g_K]^T \in (0, 1)^K$ is applied symmetrically to scale input sensor channels in both context and target encoders:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{ctx}}[:, k] = \mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}}[:, k] \cdot g_k, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{tgt}}[:, k] = \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}[:, k] \cdot g_k.
\end{equation}
Because $\mathbf{g}$ depends exclusively on $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}}$, we have:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial g_k}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}} \equiv \mathbf{0}, \quad \forall k \in \{1, \dots, K\},
\end{equation}
guaranteeing exact zero-leakage shielding: future transient spikes or anomalies in the suspect target window $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}}$ cannot artificially amplify channel weights prior to encoding. For univariate streams ($K = 1$), $s_1 = \bar{s} = 1$ and $\sigma_s = 0$, causing the gate to smoothly reduce to identity scaling ($\tilde{x}_1 = x_1$).

\paragraph{Gating Boundary Conditions and Failure Modes}
We note three operational boundary conditions inherent to cross-channel variance gating. First, if a sensor channel exhibits intrinsically small normal fluctuations (such as stable thermal or cryogenic telemetry), its variance share $s_k$ remains low, attenuating its contribution even when an anomaly manifests as an abnormal variance increase that remains small in absolute magnitude relative to noisy channels. To mitigate baseline scale disparity across heterogeneous physical sensors, all raw telemetry streams are standardized via nominal calibration statistics ($\bm{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \bm{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$) prior to gating. Second, quiet faults (such as sensor flatlines, stuck values, or loss-of-signal failures where variance drops toward zero, $v_k^{\text{ctx}} \to 0$) receive low variance scores and cannot be isolated through variance amplification. Instead, flatlines are detected through trajectory predictive discrepancy $\mathbf{e}(t) = \hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{tgt}}$, where the predictor fails to anticipate an unnatural constant state. Third, abrupt operational mode transitions (e.g., thruster firings or day-to-night orbital transitions) produce transient cross-channel heteroscedasticity that temporarily perturbs variance shares until the sliding context window $C=256$ is replenished with observations from the new regime.

Complementing input channel gating, an anti-drowning coordinate saliency gate is applied to latent error vectors $\mathbf{e} = z_{\text{tgt}} - z_{\text{pred}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ during discrepancy scoring:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\text{sal}}(\mathbf{e}) = (1 - \alpha) \|\mathbf{e}\|_2 + \alpha \sqrt{D \sum_{d=1}^D w_d e_d^2}, \quad w_d = \frac{\exp(|e_d| / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^D \exp(|e_j| / \tau)},
\end{equation}
which amplifies sparse latent coordinate deviations by up to $\sqrt{D}$ while maintaining exact equivalence to $\|\mathbf{e}\|_2$ when errors are isotropic ($e_1 = \dots = e_D$).

\subsubsection{Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Real-world physical anomalies operate across disparate temporal scales, ranging from high-frequency electrical spikes to gradual thermal drifts. We integrate Littlewood-Paley dyadic frequency decomposition into the encoder front-end. The input sequence is decomposed into $J=4$ dyadic octave bands via dyadic octave bandpass filters $\psi_j(\omega) = \psi(2^j \omega)$ satisfying the symmetric Littlewood-Paley partition of unity over $[-\pi, \pi]$:
\begin{equation}
    |\phi(\omega)|^2 + \sum_{j=0}^{J-1} |\psi(2^j \omega)|^2 = 1, \quad \forall \omega \in [-\pi, \pi].
\end{equation}
This multi-scale frequency decomposition enables the model to isolate high-frequency transient anomalies from low-frequency baseline diurnal oscillations without expanding parameter count.

\subsubsection{Kurtosis-Adaptive Extreme Value Theory (EVT) and Calibration Realities}
Following scoring, binary alarm decisions $\hat{y}_t = \mathbb{I}(\mathcal{S}(t) > \tau_t)$ are generated via Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}. In classical SPOT, extreme value theory dictates that excesses over a high initial threshold $u$ (set uniformly at the 98th percentile, $u=0.98$) asymptotically follow a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) with scale $\sigma$ and shape $\xi$:
\begin{equation}
    \tau = u + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\left[ \left(\frac{q N}{N_u}\right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right],
\end{equation}
where $q = 10^{-3}$ denotes the design risk probability, $N$ is total calibration samples, and $N_u$ is the count of excesses. To account for varying tail heaviness across diverse sensor regimes, we modulate the threshold via an empirical heuristic factor governed by the sample excess kurtosis $\gamma_2$ of calibration residuals:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau \cdot \left[ 1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh(\gamma_2) \right].
\end{equation}
On heavy-tailed distributions ($\gamma_2 > 0$), $\tau_{\text{eff}}$ increases, raising the threshold to suppress false alarms from volatile noise bursts; on light-tailed distributions ($\gamma_2 < 0$), the threshold tightens to maximize detection sensitivity.

We emphasize that this kurtosis adjustment is an engineering heuristic designed to accommodate pre-asymptotic tail behavior in finite telemetry samples, rather than a formal asymptotic theorem of extreme value theory. In our 16-cell protocol ablation (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} and Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}), isolating the kurtosis multiplier revealed that it does not improve out-of-sample risk control: on held-out data, modulating the threshold by $1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh(\gamma_2)$ increased nominal FPR (e.g., for TS-JEPA from $5.17\%$ to $18.80\%$) without improving Point-F1 ($0.1947$ vs. $0.1929$). 

In practical telemetry deployments, EVT cannot eliminate threshold calibration challenges. While the mathematical design risk is set to $q = 10^{-3}$ (which under an ideal asymptotic GPD regime would imply an expected false positive rate of 0.1\%), empirical false positive rates across real-world multi-sensor streams typically settle around $5\text{--}8\%$, representing a substantial calibration gap. This discrepancy stems from finite-sample estimation variance, temporal autocorrelation among consecutive residuals, and non-stationary distribution shifts between nominal training and operational deployment. Under thresholds fit strictly on held-out nominal data (Section~\ref{sec:exp}), the measured nominal FPR at $q=10^{-3}$ is $5.2\text{--}7.9\%$ for the JEPAs and $24\text{--}30\%$ for TimesNet and TranAD, demonstrating that EVT does not deliver its design risk for any model and that the kurtosis adjustment does not close the gap. What our investigation reveals is that latent predictive residuals yield substantially fewer false alarms than raw reconstruction residuals under identical calibration conditions, reflecting lighter residual tails at the cost of lower causal recall. In our benchmark, thresholds are calibrated using held-out nominal splits under static EVT; in operational incident analysis, dynamic rolling SPOT is evaluated to examine streaming baseline drift.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:exp}

This section outlines the experimental setup, baseline architectures, and the multi-tier statistical evaluation protocol designed to prevent metric inflation.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Hyperparameters}
\label{subsec:exp_setup}
To ensure rigorous and fair comparison, all architectures are trained and evaluated under an identical streaming time-series pipeline. Telemetry streams are processed using a sliding window with past context duration $C=256$ and suspect target horizon $S=64$ (total window length $L=320$), evaluated with a stride of 1 timestep across all testing streams.

Context representations $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ ($D=32$) are extracted using a causal dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) comprising 3 residual blocks with channel depth $F=48$, kernel size $K=5$, and dilation factors $d \in \{1, 2, 4\}$. While the convolutional receptive field across the 3 dilated blocks spans $1 + 2(K-1)\sum_l d_l = 57$ timesteps, global temporal average pooling across the resulting feature maps aggregates information across the entire context window $C=256$, condensing the sequence into a fixed-dimensional latent representation $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ regularized by Layer Normalization and dropout ($p=0.20$). The momentum target encoder parameters $\theta_{\text{tgt}}$ are updated via Exponential Moving Average (EMA) tracking: $\theta_{\text{tgt}} \leftarrow m \theta_{\text{tgt}} + (1-m) \theta_{\text{ctx}}$, where momentum coefficient $m$ follows a cosine schedule from $m_{\text{base}}=0.996$ to $m_{\text{max}}=0.9995$.

Models are optimized using AdamW with an initial learning rate $\eta=10^{-3}$ decayed to $\eta_{\text{min}}=10^{-5}$ via Cosine Annealing, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and mini-batch size $B=32$ across 20 training epochs on nominal telemetry sequences. 

\paragraph{Held-Out Calibration Protocol}
To evaluate out-of-sample threshold calibration without circularity or test-label leakage, we partition each stream's nominal telemetry into three strictly disjoint chronological blocks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training Block (first 65\%):} Used exclusively to train neural network representations and feature extraction weights.
    \item \textbf{Nominal Calibration Block (subsequent 17.5\%):} Completely withheld from model training and hyperparameter tuning. Used exclusively to compute nominal residual scores and fit stream-specific extreme-value tail parameters (Generalized Pareto Distribution scale $\sigma$ and shape $\xi$ above the 98th percentile $u=0.98$ under design risk $q=10^{-3}$). Tail parameters are fitted independently per stream and per model architecture. Sample kurtosis $\gamma_2$ is estimated strictly within this calibration split.
    \item \textbf{Nominal Evaluation Block (final 17.5\%):} Separated from the calibration block by an unobserved 64-step isolation gap to prevent window overlap. On this block, the empirical False Positive Rate (Held-Out FPR) is measured on nominal data the threshold never encountered during calibration.
\end{enumerate}
In the primary benchmark, thresholds remain fixed during test evaluation to measure the stability of static extreme-value bounds under operational distribution drift. In operational incident analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:incident_metrics}), dynamic rolling SPOT is evaluated to examine adaptive tracking of baseline drift.

\paragraph{Multi-Seed Replication and Statistical Significance}
To account for initialization variance, all principal learned models on the non-GHL benchmark streams and controlled ablations are trained across three independent random seeds (42, 123, and 456). We report mean performance, seed standard deviations, moving-block bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (preserving temporal autocorrelation within streams), and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired stream-level differences. Differences with $p > 0.05$ are treated as statistically inconclusive rather than evidence of equivalence.

\subsection{Baseline Architectures and Parameter Capacities}
To isolate algorithmic principles from external implementation discrepancies, all baselines are executed within a unified PyTorch benchmarking pipeline under identical windowing, standardization, and EVT calibration:

\noindent\textbf{TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}:} A multi-periodic reconstructive architecture that transforms 1D time series into 2D variation spaces via 1D-FFT periodic frequency discovery and 2D Inception blocks ($648{,}553$ trainable parameters at benchmark dimension $K=25$; $574{,}529$ at $K=9$).

\noindent\textbf{TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad}:} An adversarial reconstructive Transformer featuring two-stage self-conditioning and sub-series attention ($205{,}746$ trainable parameters at $K=25$; $201{,}090$ at $K=9$).

\noindent\textbf{TS-JEPA (Unconstrained Control):} A standard Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture predicting target representations $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from context $z_{\text{ctx}}$ via a 2-layer MLP predictor without physical inductive priors ($289{,}061$ trainable parameters at $K=25$; $218{,}309$ at $K=9$). Across varying channel dimensions $K$, only the initial 1D projection layer scales ($+48$ parameters per channel), while the Littlewood-Paley wavelet front-end employs fixed dyadic filter coefficients with zero learned parameters.

\noindent\textbf{Physics-Inspired Formulations:} Three candidate architectures incorporating spectral Koopman dissipation (\texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, $285{,}733$ parameters at $K=25$; $214{,}981$ at $K=9$), turbulent momentum transport (\texttt{Reynolds-Stress JEPA}, $295{,}953$ parameters at $K=25$; $225{,}201$ at $K=9$), and conservative curl-free potential constraints (\texttt{Potential-Flow JEPA}, $338{,}117$ parameters at $K=25$; $267{,}365$ at $K=9$). In \texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, parameterizing the linear Koopman transition $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = \mathbf{K} z_{\text{ctx}}$ directly via matrix $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ requires $D^2 = 1{,}024$ weights, replacing the MLP predictor and reducing overall capacity ($1.15\%$ smaller than TS-JEPA at $K=25$, and $1.5\%$ smaller at $K=9$). Reynolds-Stress introduces $+2.4\%$ parameters at $K=25$ for its covariance closure head, while Potential-Flow adds $+17.0\%$ parameters through its velocity-matching potential network. These specifications confirm that performance characteristics across models do not stem from parameter scaling discrepancies.

\subsection{Multi-Tier Statistical Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:eval_metrics}
Recent critical surveys in time-series anomaly detection \cite{kim2022towards, schmidl2022anomaly, wu2023current} demonstrate that standard evaluation protocols are plagued by severe reporting vulnerabilities, including point-adjustment inflation, channel-averaging imbalances, and arbitrary threshold gaming. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, we report evaluation metrics across multiple complementary tiers:

\noindent\textbf{Dataset-Macro Point-F1 (Primary Metric):} We compute the unweighted arithmetic mean of Point-F1 scores across datasets:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Macro-F1}_{\text{DS}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \left( \frac{1}{|C_d|} \sum_{c \in C_d} \text{F1}_{c} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of benchmark datasets and $C_d$ is the set of channels in dataset $d$. This prevents datasets with numerous channels (such as GHL with 14 streams) from dominating the aggregate benchmark score, giving equal weight to each distinct physical application domain.

\noindent\textbf{Pooled Micro Point-F1:} We pool all raw True Positives, False Positives, and False Negatives globally across all test timesteps:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Micro-F1} = \frac{2 \sum_{c} \text{TP}_c}{2 \sum_c \text{TP}_c + \sum_c \text{FP}_c + \sum_c \text{FN}_c}
\end{equation}
Micro-F1 directly penalizes models that flood long streams with hundreds of thousands of false alarms, exposing reconstructive noise drowning.

\noindent\textbf{Channel-Macro Point-F1:} The standard per-channel average $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{F1}_i$ across all evaluated channels.

\noindent\textbf{Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC):} Average Precision computed over all possible continuous anomaly score thresholds:
\begin{equation}
    \text{PR-AUC} = \sum_{k} (R_k - R_{k-1}) P_k
\end{equation}
PR-AUC evaluates raw score ranking capability entirely independent of the threshold calibration mechanism.

\noindent\textbf{Trivial All-Positive Reference Baseline:} We report the analytical performance of a constant all-positive predictor ($\hat{y}_t = 1, \forall t$):
\begin{equation}
    \text{F1}_{\text{all-pos}} = \frac{2 \cdot P}{2 \cdot P + N} = \frac{2 \pi}{1 + \pi}
\end{equation}
where $\pi = P / (P+N)$ is the anomaly base rate. Any model failing to exceed $\text{F1}_{\text{all-pos}}$ provides less discriminative value than an uncalibrated constant alarm.

\noindent\textbf{Contiguous Point-Adjusted F1 (PA-F1):} Standard segment adjustment where detecting any single anomaly point expands to the entire contiguous anomaly segment. To prevent boundary bleeding, point adjustment is computed strictly within contiguous ground-truth segments before metric calculation. We also report the \textit{Test-Sweep PA-F1} (best post-hoc threshold scan) to quantify the remaining calibration gap.

\subsection{Operational Latency: Strictly Causal Online Detection vs. Buffered Horizons}
\label{subsec:causal_vs_buffered_protocol}
A critical operational consideration in streaming telemetry monitoring is the temporal availability of observations at decision time. To prevent confounding early-warning capabilities with future-observation access, we define and evaluate two distinct operational settings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Strictly Causal Online Detection (Zero Lookahead):} At decision timestamp $T$, the detector accesses strictly historical observations up to the current time $\{X_\tau\}_{\tau \le T}$. Scores are mapped via trailing attribution, guaranteeing that alarms are generated with zero lookahead latency budget ($\text{Latency} = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Buffered Window Detection ($S=64$ Lookahead Delay):} An observation buffer of $S=64$ future timesteps is collected before evaluating target predictive consistency. This allows capturing full multi-step trajectory evolution, but introduces a deliberate operational buffering latency of $S=64$ steps.
\end{enumerate}
For both settings, we explicitly record Mean Detection Delay ($\Delta t = t_{\text{alarm}} - t_{\text{onset}}$ in timesteps from ground-truth fault inception), Event Recall within delay budgets ($\Delta t \le 10, 64$ steps), and False Alarm Rate per 1,000 timesteps ($\text{FAR}_{1\text{k}}$).

\section{Ablation Studies and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablations}

To isolate the individual contributions of each mathematical and architectural component, we conduct controlled ablation experiments across multiple telemetry regimes.

\subsection{Ablation 1: Physical Regularizer Controls on Fixed Architectures}
To test whether the proposed physics-inspired regularizers provide measurable benefits over unconstrained latent prediction, Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) reports controlled ablations on a multi-seed subset of seven benchmark streams (SMAP \texttt{P-3}, \texttt{A-1}; MSL \texttt{M-1}, \texttt{C-1}; SMD \texttt{machine-1-2}, \texttt{machine-1-3}; Daphnet \texttt{S01R01E1}; evaluated across 3 seeds under the held-out calibration protocol). In each comparison, the neural backbone is held strictly constant while the specific physical loss term or inductive module is disabled:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the spectral entropy regularizer ($\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$) leaves Point-F1 unchanged ($0.018 \pm 0.019$ vs. $0.017 \pm 0.015$, difference $+0.001$, $95\%$ bootstrap interval $[-0.006, +0.009]$, Wilcoxon $p=1.00$). Both variants maintain PR-AUC around $0.155\text{--}0.156$.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. $\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the second-moment stress loss ($\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$) yields $0.033 \pm 0.035$ Point-F1 compared to $0.017 \pm 0.012$ with the stress loss active. Scoring via the mean predictive discrepancy alone yields $0.040 \pm 0.022$. Neither difference exceeds random seed variation ($p \ge 0.375$).
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. No-Regime Control:} Disabling the Harmonic Grassmannian regime routing ($K=1$) yields $0.068 \pm 0.082$ Point-F1 compared to $0.074 \pm 0.069$ with routing active (difference $+0.006$, interval $[-0.022, +0.032]$, $p=0.58$).
\end{enumerate}
These controlled ablations demonstrate that the physical regularizers do not produce statistically detectable improvements over unconstrained latent prediction on telemetry.

\subsection{Ablation 2: Coordinate Saliency Gating in High-Dimensional Sensor Arrays}
In high-dimensional telemetry ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults typically originate in a localized subsystem (e.g., a single valve or battery cell). In standard latent pooling:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\text{pool}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K z_k
\end{equation}
the localized anomalous signal is diluted by ambient normal noise across the remaining quiescent channels. 

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b), disabling Coordinate Saliency Gating ($\tilde{x}_k = x_k$) causes a substantial drop in Point-F1 on MSL ($0.2439 \to 0.1741$) and SMAP ($0.4157 \to 0.1982$). The parameter-free saliency gate $\tilde{x}_k = x_k \cdot \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right)$ dynamically isolates high-variance, non-stationary coordinates, suppressing ambient quiescent dimensions and maintaining high signal-to-noise ratio.

\paragraph{Causal Leakage and Preprocessing Ablation}
To confirm that channel gating does not introduce target-dependent leakage, we evaluate four gating regimes on NASA SMAP ($K=25$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shared Context-Derived Gating (Ours):} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ are computed strictly from historical context and applied symmetrically to both context and target windows. This protocol guarantees strict causality ($\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$) and achieves \textbf{0.4157} Point-F1.
    \item \textbf{Target-Only Leaky Control:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ are computed from the suspect target window. This non-causal control artificially amplifies channels that spike within the target window, achieving $0.4285$ Point-F1 ($+3.1\%$ relative over causal gating), demonstrating that context-derived gating captures virtually all of the anti-drowning benefit without violating temporal causality.
    \item \textbf{Independent Gating:} Computing $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ for context and $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ for target applies asymmetric coordinate transformations, breaking latent metric alignment and reducing Point-F1 to $0.3340$.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Pooling (No Gating):} Disabling channel weighting completely yields $0.1982$ Point-F1 (a $52.3\%$ relative degradation).
\end{enumerate}
These comparisons confirm that the performance gain stems from suppressing quiescent noise rather than leaking target-window statistics.

\subsection{Ablation 3: Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Real-world industrial and environmental telemetry contains compound temporal dynamics operating simultaneously across disjoint frequency bands. For instance, in municipal water infrastructure (\texttt{GECCO}), sensors record 24-hour diurnal consumption cycles alongside sudden pressure drops and pipe contaminant inflows across multi-hour horizons.

Standard monolithic convolutions either over-smooth high-frequency transients or fail to capture long-period cycles. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(c), replacing standard convolutions with Littlewood-Paley dyadic wavelet projections:
\begin{equation}
    z^{(j)} = \Psi_j \ast x, \quad j \in \{0, 1, \dots, J-1\}
\end{equation}
improves Point-F1 on \texttt{GECCO} from $0.1429$ to $0.4040$ on \texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, while lifting Point-F1 on \texttt{SWAN} magnetograms from $0.2210$ to $0.4349$ and on \texttt{Daphnet} accelerometry from $0.0521$ to $0.0952$. Decoupling frequency octaves prevents low-frequency power from masking subtle high-frequency phase disruptions.

\subsection{Ablation 4: Extreme Value Threshold Calibration on Nominal Splits}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(d) evaluates threshold calibration rules on held-out nominal telemetry residuals. When Extreme Value Theory (SPOT) is calibrated with design risk $q=10^{-3}$, empirical false-positive rates on nominal evaluation data settle at $5.2\text{--}7.9\%$ for JEPA models and $24\text{--}30\%$ for reconstructive baselines, missing the theoretical $0.1\%$ risk target.

Simple empirical quantile rules perform similarly: a fixed 99.5th percentile rule yields an empirical nominal FPR of $6.6\text{--}7.5\%$ with Point-F1 $0.245$, and taking the validation maximum yields an FPR of $5.2\text{--}6.9\%$ with Point-F1 $0.248$. Conversely, the kurtosis-adaptive multiplier $\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau(1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh\gamma_2)$ increases held-out nominal FPR from $0.052$ to $0.188$ on TS-JEPA and from $0.062$ to $0.110$ on Operator-Entropy, without improving Point-F1. This demonstrates that parametric EVT tail calibration provides no measurable advantage over standard empirical percentiles on real-world telemetry, and kurtosis heuristics do not resolve pre-asymptotic tail drift.

\subsection{Controlled Backbone Comparison: Latent Space vs. Observation Space}
A foundational hypothesis of this investigation is that predicting dynamics in a contracted latent representation space produces residual distributions that are fundamentally better conditioned for automated extreme-value thresholding than residuals derived from raw observation-space reconstruction or forecasting. However, directly contrasting full-featured JEPA models against existing public baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD) introduces potential architectural confounding from differing receptive fields, encoder depths, normalization schemes, and pooling operations.

To eliminate these confounding factors, Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} evaluates three architectural formulations under a strictly unified \texttt{HybridTCNEncoder} backbone ($C=256, S=64$, latent dimension $d=32$, 3-layer dilated causal TCN with 48 filters, LayerNorm, GELU, and temporal pooling):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Recon:} Autoencoder encoding the full window $X_{t-C:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{(C+S)\times K}$ into $z \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding back to reconstruct the observation space $\hat{X}$. The anomaly score is the suspect-window mean squared reconstruction error.
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Pred:} Forecaster encoding context $X_{t-C:t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ into $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding directly to forecast upcoming observations $\hat{X}_{t:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$. The anomaly score is the raw observation forecasting residual.
    \item \textbf{TS-JEPA (Latent Space):} Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture encoding context to $z_{\text{ctx}}$, predicting the future latent representation $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = g_\phi(z_{\text{ctx}})$, and scoring via predictive discrepancy against target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from an EMA target encoder.
\end{enumerate}
All models are trained for 10 epochs using AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$) with batch size 16 and calibrated via Kurtosis-Adaptive EVT ($u=0.98, \alpha=10^{-3}$) on a held-out 20\% nominal validation split of the training telemetry.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports empirical evaluations and dynamical diagnostics across 33 matched continuous telemetry streams spanning seven physical sensing domains under the held-out calibration protocol. We evaluate whether forward prediction in latent representation space produces residual distributions that are better conditioned for automated thresholding, investigate the empirical behavior of physics-inspired regularizers against unconstrained latent controls, analyze operational streaming latency, and quantify the reliability of extreme-value threshold calibration on real telemetry.

Several primary empirical findings emerge from Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark}:

\noindent\textbf{False Alarm Suppression on Held-Out Telemetry:} The primary operational advantage of latent-space predictive architectures is a substantial reduction in nominal false alarms. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves an out-of-sample nominal FPR of $0.066$, Operator-Entropy achieves $0.079$, and Reynolds-Stress achieves $0.076$, compared to $0.297$ for TimesNet and $0.309$ for TranAD. Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), paired difference tests establish a statistically significant reduction in held-out FPR: TS-JEPA achieves a mean paired FPR reduction of $-0.154$ against TimesNet ($95\%$ bootstrap confidence interval $[-0.249, -0.071]$, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.008$) and $-0.157$ against TranAD ($[-0.247, -0.082]$, $p=0.001$). Similarly, Operator-Entropy achieves a paired FPR reduction of $-0.148$ against TimesNet ($[-0.236, -0.069]$, $p=0.001$) and $-0.151$ against TranAD ($[-0.250, -0.065]$, $p=0.003$). Predicting residuals in contracted latent representations avoids observation-level high-frequency noise, yielding better-conditioned score distributions.

\noindent\textbf{Point-F1 and Continuous Ranking (PR-AUC) Parity:} While latent prediction suppresses nominal false alarms, it does not provide a statistically distinguishable improvement in overall detection fidelity. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves a stream-macro Point-F1 of $0.254$ and PR-AUC of $0.333$ (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.153$), compared to $0.207$ Point-F1 and $0.336$ PR-AUC for TimesNet (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.104$), and $0.131$ Point-F1 and $0.291$ PR-AUC for TranAD (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.070$). Across all 33 streams, paired differences between TS-JEPA and TimesNet are $+0.018$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ CI $[-0.052, +0.089]$, $p=0.55$) and $+0.002$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.098, +0.097]$, $p=0.30$). Against TranAD, the paired difference is $+0.062$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.012, +0.150]$, $p=0.94$) and $+0.027$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.075, +0.135]$, $p=0.30$). Thus, continuous ranking fidelity and calibrated Point-F1 remain statistically comparable between unconstrained latent prediction and observation-space reconstructive baselines.

\noindent\textbf{Event Recall and Detection Conservatism:} The lower false-positive rate of latent predictive models is accompanied by a reduction in event recall. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TimesNet captures $58.6\%$ of ground-truth anomaly events, and TranAD captures $48.1\%$, whereas TS-JEPA captures $49.8\%$, Reynolds-Stress captures $39.8\%$, and Potential-Flow captures $33.1\%$. Latent-space residuals act as a more conservative detector: by filtering high-frequency variations, subtle anomaly onsets that do not produce large latent coordinate shifts are missed, suppressing false alarms at the expense of lower event sensitivity.

\subsection{Evaluating Physical Inductive Priors: Hypothesis Testing and Null Results}
\label{subsec:eval_physics_priors}

A central hypothesis evaluated in this investigation is whether incorporating physics-inspired inductive regularizers into latent representations (Koopman spectral entropy, Reynolds stress closure, and potential-flow curl-free dynamics) improves anomaly detection performance over an unconstrained latent predictive architecture (\texttt{TS-JEPA}).

The empirical data across all 33 streams refute this hypothesis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. TS-JEPA:} Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), Operator-Entropy yields a mean paired difference of $-0.007$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ bootstrap CI $[-0.028, +0.012]$, Wilcoxon $p=0.71$) and $+0.008$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.013, +0.034]$, $p=0.28$). Held-out nominal FPR difference is $+0.006$ ($[-0.024, +0.042]$, $p=0.39$). Across 33 streams, Operator-Entropy outperforms TS-JEPA in Point-F1 on only 9 streams.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. TS-JEPA:} Reynolds-Stress yields a paired difference of $-0.026$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.070, +0.004]$, $p=0.33$) and a statistically significant reduction in PR-AUC ($-0.009$, $[-0.024, +0.007]$, $p=0.013$). Held-out FPR difference is $+0.003$ ($[-0.026, +0.040]$, $p=0.94$). Reynolds-Stress leads TS-JEPA in F1 on 11 of 33 streams.
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. TS-JEPA:} Potential-Flow performs significantly worse than unconstrained TS-JEPA across both calibrated Point-F1 (paired difference $-0.091$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.178, -0.019]$, $p=0.020$) and continuous ranking PR-AUC (paired difference $-0.076$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.142, -0.019]$, $p=0.039$). Potential-Flow outperforms TS-JEPA on only 5 of 33 streams.
\end{enumerate}

These findings are corroborated by the matched-capacity regularizer controls reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a). When the underlying neural architecture is held fixed on the seven-stream multi-seed subset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Disabling the spectral entropy loss ($\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$) changes Point-F1 by $+0.001$ ($[-0.006, +0.009]$, $p=1.00$).
    \item Disabling the Reynolds stress loss ($\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$), or scoring via predictive mean error alone, produces differences that do not exceed seed noise ($p \ge 0.375$).
    \item Disabling Grassmannian regime routing in Potential-Flow changes Point-F1 by $+0.006$ ($[-0.022, +0.032]$, $p=0.58$).
\end{itemize}
We conclude that the physical regularizers do not provide a measurable anomaly detection advantage over unconstrained latent prediction. Whatever performance differences exist between models arise from shared representation conditioning rather than the physical loss terms.

\subsection{Automated Threshold Calibration Realities on Non-Stationary Telemetry}
\label{subsec:calibration_realities}

A critical requirement for autonomous telemetry monitoring is whether automated extreme-value thresholding adheres to theoretical design risk parameters. When practitioners specify a design risk of $q=10^{-3}$ ($0.1\%$), the operational expectation is that nominal false alarms will occur at or below this frequency.

\input{sections/tab_calibrator_comparison.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison} reports a controlled comparison of threshold calibrators evaluated on identical score streams across the 33 continuous telemetry streams under buffered inference. The empirical results demonstrate several key realities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic Design-Risk Overshoot:} Extreme-value thresholding via SPOT ($u=0.98, q=10^{-3}$) systematically fails to achieve its design risk on out-of-sample nominal telemetry. Across all 33 streams, empirical nominal FPR averages $5.17\%$ for TS-JEPA, $6.15\%$ for Operator-Entropy, $23.88\%$ for TimesNet, and $24.59\%$ for TranAD, overshooting the theoretical $0.1\%$ target by $50$-fold to $245$-fold.
    \item \textbf{High Failure Rate Across Streams:} The 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals for held-out nominal FPR strictly exclude the theoretical design risk $q=10^{-3}$ in $57\%$ of TS-JEPA runs, $65\%$ of Operator-Entropy runs, $87\%$ of TimesNet runs, and $84\%$ of TranAD runs. Real-world telemetry exhibits non-stationary baseline drift and seasonal fluctuations that violate the stationary tail assumptions of Extreme Value Theory.
    \item \textbf{Parity with Simple Empirical Quantiles:} Simple non-parametric quantile rules match or exceed the performance of SPOT. On TS-JEPA, taking the validation maximum yields an empirical held-out FPR of $0.0524$ and Point-F1 of $0.1922$, while an empirical 99.5th percentile rule yields an FPR of $0.0657$ and Point-F1 of $0.1942$. Parametric GPD tail modeling provides no demonstrable benefit over simple empirical percentiles on these benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{Failure of Kurtosis-Adaptive Calibration:} The kurtosis-adaptive multiplier rule ($\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau(1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh\gamma_2)$) substantially degrades nominal calibration. On TS-JEPA, it inflates held-out nominal FPR from $0.0517$ to $0.1880$ (with CI excluding $q$ rising from $57\%$ to $84\%$), while yielding an indistinguishable Point-F1 ($0.1947$ vs.\ $0.1929$). On Operator-Entropy, it raises nominal FPR from $0.0615$ to $0.1098$. Excess kurtosis measured in training splits does not accurately forecast non-stationary tail shifts on out-of-sample telemetry.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we conducted an empirical investigation into whether Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) and physics-inspired inductive regularizers resolve the false-alarm vulnerability of multivariate time-series anomaly detection. By evaluating 33 continuous telemetry streams across seven physical sensing domains under a strictly held-out calibration protocol, this study yields three primary conclusions:

First, forward prediction in a contracted latent representation space fundamentally conditions residual distributions. By filtering out localized observation-space sensor jitter, latent prediction reduces test residual excess kurtosis and achieves a statistically significant 4-fold to 5-fold reduction in out-of-sample nominal false-positive rates compared to reconstructive baselines (paired difference $-0.154$ against TimesNet, $p=0.008$; $-0.157$ against TranAD, $p=0.001$). However, continuous ranking (PR-AUC) and calibrated Point-F1 remain statistically indistinguishable between unconstrained latent prediction and baselines ($p \ge 0.30$). Under strictly causal streaming, latent prediction suppresses false alarms to $0.47\%\text{--}4.18\%$, but at the cost of lower point-level F1 and delayed event recall.

Second, empirical hypothesis testing refutes the claim that physics-inspired regularizers provide an anomaly detection advantage over unconstrained latent prediction. Across all 33 streams, neither Operator-Entropy (spectral rank preservation) nor Reynolds-Stress (second-moment fluctuation alignment) outperforms unconstrained TS-JEPA ($p \ge 0.33$), while Potential-Flow (curl-free gradient dynamics) performs significantly worse ($p=0.020$) due to its inability to represent circulating limit-cycle orbits. In matched-capacity controls holding the neural backbone fixed, disabling each physical loss term changes Point-F1 by $+0.001$ to $+0.006$ ($p \ge 0.58$). While diagnostic experiments confirm that these formulations enforce their intended mathematical properties, real-world telemetry deviations do not obey idealized physical conservation laws, rendering these regularizers ineffective as general anomaly detection priors.

Third, automated threshold calibration via Extreme Value Theory (SPOT) systematically overshoots its theoretical design risk on non-stationary telemetry. At $q=10^{-3}$, empirical nominal false-positive rates settle at $5.2\%\text{--}7.4\%$ for JEPAs and $23.9\%\text{--}24.6\%$ for reconstructive baselines, with 95\% bootstrap intervals excluding $q$ in 57\%--87\% of runs. Parametric EVT tail modeling provides no measurable advantage over simple empirical quantiles, and kurtosis adjustments fail to correct pre-asymptotic tail drift.

Four directions extend this investigation. First, incorporating solenoidal vorticity components into velocity fields via Helmholtz decomposition enables modeling dissipative systems with limit-cycle attractors without sacrificing conservative stability. Second, developing memory-bounded online drift adaptation will allow tracking diurnal thermal cycles and operational setpoint transitions without buffer contamination. Third, implementing accelerated sliding-window inference kernels will enable scaling multi-seed evaluations to million-step telemetry streams. Finally, quantizing JEPA encoders into 8-bit integer formats will support deployment on radiation-hardened satellite microcontrollers and low-power industrial edge hardware.
