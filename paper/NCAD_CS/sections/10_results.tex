\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports empirical evaluations and dynamical diagnostics across 33 matched continuous telemetry streams spanning seven physical sensing domains under the held-out calibration protocol. We evaluate whether forward prediction in latent representation space produces residual distributions that are better conditioned for automated thresholding, investigate the empirical behavior of physics-inspired regularizers against unconstrained latent controls, analyze operational streaming latency, and quantify the reliability of extreme-value threshold calibration on real telemetry.

\subsection{Benchmark Performance on Continuous Telemetry}
\label{subsec:core_benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} presents the empirical comparison across 33 continuous telemetry streams evaluated under the held-out calibration protocol. Models are trained on the first 65\% of each nominal sequence, and extreme-value thresholds (SPOT, $u=0.98, q=10^{-3}$) are calibrated on a strictly held-out 17.5\% nominal segment, with out-of-sample nominal False-Positive Rate (Held-Out FPR) evaluated on the remaining nominal 17.5\% segment (isolated by a 64-step gap). Figure~\ref{fig:hero_sota_comparison} illustrates the comparative profile across strict Point-F1, continuous ranking (PR-AUC), and held-out nominal FPR.

\begin{figure*}[t]

\caption{Held-Out Calibration Benchmark across 33 continuous telemetry streams (Non-GHL stream-macro means averaged across three seeds). Latent-space predictive architectures achieve a 4-fold to 5-fold reduction in held-out nominal False-Positive Rate compared to reconstructive baselines, while maintaining comparable Point-F1 and PR-AUC.\label{fig:hero_sota_comparison}}

Several primary empirical findings emerge from Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark}:

\noindent\textbf{False Alarm Suppression on Held-Out Telemetry:} The primary operational advantage of latent-space predictive architectures is a substantial reduction in nominal false alarms. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves an out-of-sample nominal FPR of $0.066$, Operator-Entropy achieves $0.079$, and Reynolds-Stress achieves $0.076$, compared to $0.297$ for TimesNet and $0.309$ for TranAD. Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), paired difference tests establish a statistically significant reduction in held-out FPR: TS-JEPA achieves a mean paired FPR reduction of $-0.154$ against TimesNet ($95\%$ bootstrap confidence interval $[-0.249, -0.071]$, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.008$) and $-0.157$ against TranAD ($[-0.247, -0.082]$, $p=0.001$). Similarly, Operator-Entropy achieves a paired FPR reduction of $-0.148$ against TimesNet ($[-0.236, -0.069]$, $p=0.001$) and $-0.151$ against TranAD ($[-0.250, -0.065]$, $p=0.003$). Predicting residuals in contracted latent representations avoids observation-level high-frequency noise, yielding better-conditioned score distributions (as isolated directly under the same-backbone ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_backbone} and Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone}).

\noindent\textbf{Point-F1 and Continuous Ranking (PR-AUC) Parity:} While latent prediction suppresses nominal false alarms, it does not provide a statistically distinguishable improvement in overall detection fidelity. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TS-JEPA achieves a stream-macro Point-F1 of $0.254$ and PR-AUC of $0.333$ (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.153$), compared to $0.207$ Point-F1 and $0.336$ PR-AUC for TimesNet (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.104$), and $0.131$ Point-F1 and $0.291$ PR-AUC for TranAD (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.070$). Across all 33 streams, paired differences between TS-JEPA and TimesNet are $+0.018$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ CI $[-0.052, +0.089]$, $p=0.55$) and $+0.002$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.098, +0.097]$, $p=0.30$). Against TranAD, the paired difference is $+0.062$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.012, +0.150]$, $p=0.94$) and $+0.027$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.075, +0.135]$, $p=0.30$). Thus, continuous ranking fidelity and calibrated Point-F1 remain statistically comparable between unconstrained latent prediction and observation-space reconstructive baselines.

\noindent\textbf{Event Recall and Detection Conservatism:} The lower false-positive rate of latent predictive models is accompanied by a reduction in event recall. On the 19 non-GHL streams, TimesNet captures $58.6\%$ of ground-truth anomaly events, and TranAD captures $48.1\%$, whereas TS-JEPA captures $49.8\%$, Reynolds-Stress captures $39.8\%$, and Potential-Flow captures $33.1\%$. Latent-space residuals act as a more conservative detector: by filtering high-frequency variations, subtle anomaly onsets that do not produce large latent coordinate shifts are missed, suppressing false alarms at the expense of lower event sensitivity.

\noindent\textbf{Detection Delay Trade-Offs ($\Delta t$):} Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} also reports the mean detection delay $\Delta t$ in timesteps from fault inception to the earliest alarm across detected events. Reconstructive baselines respond more rapidly to raw observation anomalies: TimesNet averages a detection delay of $\Delta t = 37.8$ steps, and TranAD averages $42.4$ steps. In comparison, TS-JEPA incurs a mean delay of $89.6$ steps, while Operator-Entropy ($129.9$ steps) and Reynolds-Stress ($129.2$ steps) exhibit longer response latencies. Because latent encoders smooth out high-frequency observation noise, alarms trigger only after anomalous deviations propagate into the dominant coordinates of representation space. Operational deployments must therefore weigh the 4-fold to 5-fold reduction in nominal false alarms against an increase in initial detection latency.

\noindent\textbf{Necessity of Nonlinear Representation Learning (Linear AR Control):} To establish whether non-neural linear dynamics suffice for residual conditioning, Table~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} includes a ridge-regularized Linear Autoregressive Forecaster. Under the identical held-out calibration protocol, Linear AR achieves an exceptionally low nominal FPR of $0.006$ and zero detection delay ($\Delta t = 0.0$ steps) on the events it captures. However, its calibrated Point-F1 collapses to $0.057$ (dataset-macro Point-F1 of $0.038$), capturing only $47.8\%$ of events with a point precision of just $4.35\%$. Because an observation-space linear model lacks the capacity to capture cross-channel interactions and temporal non-linearities, its residual scores fail to discriminate subtle operational faults from background variance. This non-neural baseline confirms that trivial linear forecasting cannot deliver viable anomaly detection, establishing that nonlinear latent representation learning is necessary to combine controlled false-alarm rates with usable detection accuracy.

\subsection{Evaluating Physical Inductive Priors: Hypothesis Testing and Null Results}
\label{subsec:eval_physics_priors}

A central hypothesis evaluated in this investigation is whether incorporating physics-inspired inductive regularizers into latent representations (Koopman spectral entropy, Reynolds stress closure, and potential-flow curl-free dynamics) improves anomaly detection performance over an unconstrained latent predictive architecture (\texttt{TS-JEPA}).

The empirical data across all 33 streams refute this hypothesis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. TS-JEPA:} Across all 33 streams (seeds averaged), Operator-Entropy yields a mean paired difference of $-0.007$ in Point-F1 ($95\%$ bootstrap CI $[-0.028, +0.012]$, Wilcoxon $p=0.71$) and $+0.008$ in PR-AUC ($[-0.013, +0.034]$, $p=0.28$). Held-out nominal FPR difference is $+0.006$ ($[-0.024, +0.042]$, $p=0.39$). Across 33 streams, Operator-Entropy outperforms TS-JEPA in Point-F1 on only 9 streams.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. TS-JEPA:} Reynolds-Stress yields a paired difference of $-0.026$ in Point-F1 ($[-0.070, +0.004]$, $p=0.33$) and a statistically significant reduction in PR-AUC ($-0.009$, $[-0.024, +0.007]$, $p=0.013$). Held-out FPR difference is $+0.003$ ($[-0.026, +0.040]$, $p=0.94$). Reynolds-Stress leads TS-JEPA in F1 on 11 of 33 streams.
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. TS-JEPA:} Potential-Flow performs significantly worse than unconstrained TS-JEPA across both calibrated Point-F1 (paired difference $-0.091$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.178, -0.019]$, $p=0.020$) and continuous ranking PR-AUC (paired difference $-0.076$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.142, -0.019]$, $p=0.039$). Potential-Flow outperforms TS-JEPA on only 5 of 33 streams.
\end{enumerate}

These findings align with the matched-capacity regularizer controls reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) and Section~\ref{subsec:controlled_ablations}: holding the neural backbone constant across all 19 non-GHL benchmark streams while disabling the spectral entropy loss ($\Delta F_1 = -0.003, p=0.62$) or Reynolds stress loss ($\Delta F_1 = +0.005, p=0.41$) produces changes well within seed noise ($p \ge 0.408$). We conclude that the physical regularizers provide no measurable anomaly detection advantage over unconstrained latent prediction. Performance differences between models stem from shared representation conditioning rather than the physical loss terms.

\subsection{Automated Threshold Calibration Realities on Non-Stationary Telemetry}
\label{subsec:calibration_realities}

A critical requirement for autonomous telemetry monitoring is whether automated extreme-value thresholding adheres to theoretical design risk parameters. When practitioners specify a design risk of $q=10^{-3}$ ($0.1\%$), the operational expectation is that nominal false alarms will occur at or below this frequency.

\input{sections/tab_calibrator_comparison.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison} reports a controlled comparison of threshold calibrators evaluated on identical score streams across the 33 continuous telemetry streams under buffered inference. The empirical results demonstrate several key realities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic Design-Risk Overshoot:} Extreme-value thresholding via SPOT ($u=0.98, q=10^{-3}$) systematically fails to achieve its design risk on out-of-sample nominal telemetry. Across all 33 streams, empirical nominal FPR averages $5.17\%$ for TS-JEPA, $6.15\%$ for Operator-Entropy, $23.88\%$ for TimesNet, and $24.59\%$ for TranAD, overshooting the theoretical $0.1\%$ target by $50$-fold to $245$-fold.
    \item \textbf{High Failure Rate Across Streams:} The 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals for held-out nominal FPR strictly exclude the theoretical design risk $q=10^{-3}$ in $57\%$ of TS-JEPA runs, $65\%$ of Operator-Entropy runs, $87\%$ of TimesNet runs, and $84\%$ of TranAD runs. Real-world telemetry exhibits non-stationary baseline drift and seasonal fluctuations that violate the stationary tail assumptions of Extreme Value Theory.
    \item \textbf{Parity with Simple Empirical Quantiles:} Simple non-parametric quantile rules match or exceed the performance of SPOT. On TS-JEPA, taking the validation maximum yields an empirical held-out FPR of $0.0524$ and Point-F1 of $0.1922$, while an empirical 99.5th percentile rule yields an FPR of $0.0657$ and Point-F1 of $0.1942$. Parametric GPD tail modeling provides no demonstrable benefit over simple empirical percentiles on these benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{Failure of Kurtosis-Adaptive Calibration:} The kurtosis-adaptive multiplier rule ($\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau(1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh\gamma_2)$) substantially degrades nominal calibration. On TS-JEPA, it inflates held-out nominal FPR from $0.0517$ to $0.1880$ (with CI excluding $q$ rising from $57\%$ to $84\%$), while yielding an indistinguishable Point-F1 ($0.1947$ vs.\ $0.1929$). On Operator-Entropy, it raises nominal FPR from $0.0615$ to $0.1098$. Excess kurtosis measured in training splits does not accurately forecast non-stationary tail shifts on out-of-sample telemetry.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:evt_sensitivity} provides a multi-parameter sensitivity analysis of the extreme-value calibration procedure across varying initial tail thresholds $u$, theoretical design risk levels $q$, and kurtosis-adaptive coefficients $\beta$. Three key findings emerge:
First, varying the initial threshold from $u=0.95$ to $u=0.99$ produces negligible changes in out-of-sample test FPR ($17.27\%\text{--}17.36\%$) and Point-F1 ($0.1028\text{--}0.1030$), indicating that GPD tail estimates are stable with respect to initial tail percentile selection.
Second, scaling the theoretical design risk $q$ by two orders of magnitude (from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$) shifts validation FPR from $0.0074$ to $0.00009$, but test FPR shifts by less than $1.7\%$ (from $18.95\%$ to $17.27\%$), demonstrating that operational distribution drift completely dominates the theoretical design parameter.
Third, increasing the kurtosis adjustment coefficient $\beta$ systematically degrades performance: at $\beta=0.083$, held-out validation FPR jumps from $0.0019$ to $0.2778$, and at $\beta=0.50$, it collapses to $0.5403$ (test FPR $60.96\%$), flooding the system with alarms. Non-parametric validation rules (such as Max-of-Validation and Empirical 99.5th Percentile) achieve comparable or superior nominal calibration ($0.0000\text{--}0.0054$ validation FPR) without requiring parametric tail assumptions.

\input{sections/tab_evt_failure_decomposition.tex}

To diagnose why extreme-value calibration breaks down on operational telemetry, Table~\ref{tab:evt_failure_decomposition} isolates four statistical mechanisms across benchmark telemetry streams:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Residual Autocorrelation and Extremal Clustering:} The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem \cite{balkema1974residual} requires mutually independent tail exceedances. However, reconstructive observation residuals exhibit serial correlation, with lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho_1 = 0.983 \pm 0.014$ for TimesNet and $\rho_1 = 0.990 \pm 0.007$ for TranAD. Using the Ferro-Segers extremal index estimator \cite{ferro2003inference}, observation exceedances form persistent temporal clusters with mean cluster sizes of $1/\theta = 12.7$ to $13.3$ steps (reaching $45.0$ steps on GECCO water quality telemetry). Clustering collapses the effective independent tail sample size ($N_{\text{eff}} = \theta N_u$) from $N_u = 77\text{--}91$ observations down to $N_{\text{eff}} \approx 2\text{--}6$ independent clusters. In contrast, latent prediction compresses lag-1 autocorrelation to $\rho_1 = 0.820 \pm 0.135$ and mean cluster size to $5.2 \pm 5.8$ steps.
    \item \textbf{Non-Stationary Distribution Drift:} Non-stationarity drives a wedge between calibration and held-out evaluation splits. Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests reveal substantial drift in reconstructive models ($D_{\text{KS}} = 0.357 \pm 0.178$ for TimesNet; $D_{\text{KS}} = 0.388 \pm 0.284$ for TranAD; $p < 10^{-13}$ across streams). This drift shifts the 98th percentile of nominal residuals by $+470.8\%$ on TimesNet and $+48.6\%$ on TranAD between splits. Conversely, TS-JEPA maps temporal sequences into an abstract representation space where environmental shifts are compressed, yielding less than half the KS distance ($D_{\text{KS}} = 0.154 \pm 0.069$) and near-zero relative tail drift ($\Delta q_{98} = -0.36\% \pm 14.1\%$).
    \item \textbf{GPD Goodness-of-Fit Degradation:} Cram{\'e}r-von Mises tests for Generalized Pareto tail convergence reject the stationary GPD null hypothesis ($p < 0.05$) across autocorrelated observation streams. Because parameter estimation fits a stationary tail model to non-stationary, autocorrelated bursts, parametric SPOT thresholds provide no advantage over simple empirical validation percentiles.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Risk Overshoot:} Against the specified design risk $q = 10^{-3}$ ($0.1\%$), TimesNet generates a held-out nominal FPR of $4.32\% \pm 7.60\%$ ($43.2\times$ overshoot), and TranAD produces $4.76\% \pm 10.43\%$ ($47.6\times$ overshoot). By suppressing residual drift and tail clustering, TS-JEPA curbs the empirical nominal FPR to $0.33\% \pm 0.59\%$ ($3.3\times$ overshoot), reducing the discrepancy between theoretical risk and operational deployment by an order of magnitude.
\end{enumerate}
