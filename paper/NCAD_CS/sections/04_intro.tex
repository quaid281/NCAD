\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Spacecraft, chemical plants, server farms, and wearable medical devices all report their health through dozens of sensor channels that never stop streaming \cite{meng2019spacecraft, chalapathy2019deep, cuellar2024explainable, Hundman2018b}. Somewhere in that stream a fault begins: a valve sticks, a reaction wheel drifts, a patient's gait freezes. An automated monitor has to notice before people do, and it has to learn what healthy behavior looks like from normal operating history alone, because real faults are rare and seldom labeled in advance. It also has to stay quiet the rest of the time. A monitor that cries wolf every few minutes is soon ignored, and an ignored alarm protects no one.

\noindent\emph{Why Today's Detectors Flood the Console:} Leading deep detectors such as TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}, TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad}, and Anomaly Transformer \cite{xu2022anomaly} learn to reconstruct the raw sensor signal and raise an alarm wherever the reconstruction misses. Raw telemetry, however, is full of noise that no model can predict: sensor jitter, thermal drift, and channels recorded on very different scales. Summed across 25 to 55 channels, these small misses form an error signal with frequent extreme spikes (Table~\ref{tab:terms}). We calibrate each detector on healthy data with an automated tail rule, Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}, and then run it on later healthy data, exactly as a deployed system would. The reconstructors raise false alarms during $22\%$ to $31\%$ of normal operation. At that rate the alarms carry almost no information: a random alerter firing just as often would catch $60.0\%$ of real anomaly events, slightly more than the $58.8\%$ that TimesNet catches.

\noindent\emph{The Hidden Weak Link: One Score, One Threshold:} A more promising idea is to stop predicting the noise. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) \cite{lecun2022path, carmona2021neural} compress each window of telemetry into a short summary of 32 numbers and predict the summary of what comes next instead of every raw reading. Because the summary keeps the structure of the signal and discards most of the jitter, false alarms fall by an order of magnitude, to $0.6\%\text{--}2.4\%$ of healthy operation. Yet latent detectors keep one habit from their predecessors. At the final step they add all of their prediction errors into a single anomaly score and sound the alarm when that score crosses a threshold. Our analysis shows that this last step is where much of the signal is lost. If one or two of the 32 summary coordinates drift slightly between training and calibration, as routinely happens in long-running systems, they dominate the sum. We call these coordinates \emph{hijackers}. The threshold must rise to tolerate them, and genuine faults on the remaining 30 coordinates no longer clear it. Real-time (causal) Point-F1, a standard measure that balances missed and false detections at every time step, falls to $0.050\text{--}0.052$, and faults go unnoticed for more than 240 steps.

\noindent\emph{Replacing the Threshold with a Circuit-Breaker Panel:} We remove that final step. Instead of one score and one threshold, our model, \textit{Multi-Horizon Reynolds JEPA}, reports to a panel of 224 separately calibrated circuit breakers. The analogy is the breaker box in a building: a fault on one circuit trips its own switch. To build the panel we borrow an idea from fluid mechanics, by analogy only. Reynolds decomposition \cite{reynolds1895dynamical} splits a turbulent flow into a steady mean current and the fluctuations around it. We apply the same split to each of the 32 prediction errors as it evolves over four nested look-ahead horizons of 16, 32, 48, and 64 steps, giving seven views of every coordinate: its error at each of the four horizons, its average drift, its fluctuation, and its acceleration at the newest edge of the window. Each of the resulting $7 \times 32 = 224$ dials has its own breaker, set from healthy calibration data. In our flagship variant, \textit{Laminar-JEPA}, surgical weight-space quenching projects mean calibration drift out of the predictor biases on the latent sphere and tempers precision-matrix directions that amplify out-of-sample variance, restoring a smooth nominal baseline while nominal hijack guards damp dials that had already drifted. An alarm sounds when enough breakers trip together, or at once when one of 32 fast onset relays sees a coordinate's newest horizon and its acceleration dial trip simultaneously.

\subsection{Contributions}
We evaluate the approach on 141 telemetry streams from 12 public datasets spanning spacecraft, industrial plants, server clusters, and wearables. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{A breaker panel and laminar weight-space quenching in place of a thresholded score:} We replace the scalar anomaly score and threshold with a 224-bit circuit-breaker panel built from Reynolds-decomposed latent errors, surgical weight-space quenching, nominal hijack guards, and 32 onset relays (Sections~\ref{subsec:reynolds_expansion}--\ref{subsubsec:circuit_breaker_panel}). On 19 primary streams, the panel raises causal Point-F1 from $0.052$ to $0.138$ ($17/0$ wins, $p = 1.5{\times}10^{-5}$), while \emph{Laminar-JEPA} reaches $0.262$ buffered Point-F1, $1.6\%$ causal false-positive rate (FPR), and a $59.6$-step causal delay. Across $162$ streams ($15$ datasets), \emph{Laminar-JEPA} cuts causal held-out FPR by $8.0\%$ relative ($p = 0.0011$, median $1.03\%$) and test FPR by $4.8\%$ ($p < 10^{-4}$, median $1.59\%$); on $118$ untouched streams, a three-horizon variant raises causal Point-F1 from $0.072\text{--}0.083$ to $0.143$ ($p = 1.6{\times}10^{-13}$) at $72.8\%$ recall (Tables~\ref{tab:ablations}--\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}).
    \item \emph{Where the gains come from:} Matched-size ablations ($272\text{k}\text{--}312\text{k}$ parameters) trace each step. A layer-normalization (LayerNorm) \cite{ba2016layernormalization} bottleneck tames extreme error spikes, cutting excess kurtosis $\gamma_2$ from $6.10$ to $0.91$; predicting learned summaries instead of raw readings halves calibration drift; and barycenter centering cuts the share of false alarms caused by regime drift from $71.3\%$ to $18.7\%$. A six-step ladder from one dial to 224, together with leave-one-out tests, isolates each dial family, the onset relays, prefix routing, and the hijack guards (Table~\ref{tab:ablations}).
    \item \emph{Fewer false alarms and better recall at a fixed alarm budget:} On the 19 primary streams, latent prediction holds causal FPRs at $0.6\%\text{--}1.7\%$, against $22.2\%\text{--}24.3\%$ for reconstructors. When every detector receives the same $1\%$ false-alarm budget (set post hoc from test labels, as a diagnostic), the panel catches $58.4\%$ of anomaly events versus $21.7\%$ for TimesNet, and on five NASA in-range contextual sequences it reaches $0.658$ buffered precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) (Tables~\ref{tab:core_benchmark} and~\ref{tab:point_vs_contextual}, Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_and_horizon}).
    \item \emph{Limits, theory, and latency:} A sensor that freezes silently barely moves the latent summary, so the panel alone catches only $25.0\%\text{--}29.2\%$ of such events and a rolling variance monitor is needed to reach $44.4\%\text{--}52.8\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:regularizer_and_quiet_fault}); slow process ramps remain undetected. Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse} bounds a spectral concentration ratio of the horizon fluctuations to $[\frac{1}{M-1}, 1]$, reweighting a scalar score by it gives no measurable benefit, the per-dial quantiles need about $10^3$ calibration steps, and short horizons ($S \in \{1, 4\}$) remove the 63-step reporting lag for 1D latent scoring (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_and_horizon}(a)).
\end{enumerate}
