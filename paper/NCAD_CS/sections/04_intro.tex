\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Spacecraft, power plants, and industrial machines stream readings from dozens of sensors at once \cite{meng2019spacecraft, chalapathy2019deep, cuellar2024explainable, Hundman2018b}. A detector watching this telemetry has two jobs: raise an alarm soon after something breaks, and stay quiet the rest of the time. Faults are rare and seldom labeled, so the detector must learn what normal looks like from healthy history alone. Operators lose trust quickly when a monitor alarms several times a day on healthy data, and an ignored monitor catches nothing.

The most common approach trains a network to reproduce raw sensor signals and raises an alarm when the reproduction is poor; TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}, TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad}, and Anomaly Transformer \cite{xu2022anomaly} follow this recipe. In practice, much of raw telemetry cannot be copied at all. Random sensor jitter leaves an irreducible error, and benign thermal drift or duty-cycle changes degrade the copy even when nothing is broken. Summing errors across 25--55 channels measured in different units allows one noisy channel to dominate, producing heavy-tailed spikes on healthy data.

These spikes defeat threshold calibration. Setting thresholds on healthy windows with Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly} still yields $22\%\text{--}24\%$ causal false-positive rates (FPR) for reconstruction detectors on later healthy data across our 19 design-stage development streams, roughly one step in four. Because faults span many steps, alarming at random $24\%$ of the time already hits $60.0\%$ of fault episodes; TimesNet catches $58.8\%$, reflecting frequent alarms rather than selective fault recognition.

A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) \cite{lecun2022path, carmona2021neural} avoids copying raw signals. An encoder compresses each window into a 32-number summary, and a predictor forecasts the summary of the next window. Discarding unpredictable jitter yields lighter error tails and cuts healthy false alarms on those development streams by an order of magnitude (Contribution~3).

Standard JEPA detectors, however, still collapse the 32 prediction errors into one scalar score and one threshold. When one or two summary features drift slightly as a machine warms up or switches load, their growing errors push the shared threshold upward. A fault that shifts the remaining 30 features moderately then stays below that raised threshold and goes missed. We call these drifting features \emph{hijacker dials}. On our development streams, single-score JEPAs reach causal Point-F1 (step-level alarm accuracy) of only $0.050\text{--}0.055$ and need $138\text{--}154$ steps to flag a fault.

Two evaluation shortcuts also inflate published numbers: point adjustment credits an entire fault segment if one step inside it is flagged \cite{kim2022towards}, and thresholds tuned with test labels or calibrated on adaptation data understate false alarms. We score every step without point adjustment, fit thresholds on healthy data only, separate model fitting, checkpoint selection, adaptation, and calibration into disjoint healthy blocks on our primary archive, and reserve 118 streams never inspected during design.

We replace the single scalar alarm with a panel of circuit breakers, like a building breaker box where a short in one circuit trips its own switch regardless of total load. Across target prefixes of 16, 32, and 64 steps, each of the 32 coordinates receives separate \emph{dials} tracking horizon error size and persistent drift, each with its own healthy-calibrated threshold, and the system alarms when a quorum of dials trips together. Drifting dials widen only their own thresholds instead of blinding the rest of the panel. Targeting abrupt to medium-onset faults (within roughly one $256$-step context window), we study two variants (Fig.~\ref{fig:triad_architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite}, our preferred operational architecture, predicts three horizons in parallel behind one layer-normalization (LayerNorm) step \cite{ba2016layernormalization} and monitors $4\times32=128$ position-plus-drift dials under one quorum ($35\%\text{--}52\%$ fewer parameters and $2.43\times$ faster inference).
    \item \emph{Laminar-JEPA}, a 224-dial ablation reference variant, scores four views of each coordinate's error plus 32 onset relays and optional weight editing to isolate which views aid live causal versus buffered attribution; more dials do not imply a better operational detector.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
Thirty-three development streams (where adaptation and thresholds share calibration data) provide exploratory design-stage evidence, while 118 untouched archive streams across 9 datasets (of 141 streams across 12 datasets) under a disjoint five-block protocol provide primary evidence of generalization and calibration (Section~\ref{subsec:cohort_repro}). We report live \emph{causal} streaming separately from \emph{buffered} after-the-fact attribution:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{Event coverage, buffered accuracy, and archive false-alarm behavior:} On the 118 untouched streams, \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite} raises buffered event recall (ER) from $59.0\%$ to $67.4\%$ and buffered Point-F1 from $0.143$ to $0.156$ ($p=0.022$) over a single-score multi-horizon JEPA while lowering held-out buffered FPR ($19.5\%\to 14.3\%$), retaining a $+10.9$-point recall lead at a matched $5\%$ test FPR. Under live causal reporting, it raises ER from $52.5\%$ to $63.8\%$ across all $9$ datasets (sign-flip $p=0.0039$; all $12$ leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) folds), halves delay ($70.8$ vs.\ $133.5$ steps), and beats system-matched coordinate aggregators. Pooled causal Point-F1 rises modestly from $0.076$ to $0.079$ ($95\%$ stream confidence interval (CI) $[-0.019,+0.023]$, $p=0.014$), doubling from $0.040$ to $0.079$ ($p=2.7\times 10^{-5}$) on adequately calibrated streams ($N_{\text{cal}}\ge 500$, $n=59$). Archive causal held-out FPR is right-skewed: median $0.78\%$ ($0.61\%$ for $N_{\text{cal}}\ge 500$), $87.3\%$ of streams $\le 5\%$, and mean $7.0\%$ driven by short calibration histories.
    \item \emph{Contributing factors:} Design-stage matched-backbone ablations indicate that LayerNorm and barycenter centering contribute to lighter tails and less drift under these protocols, whereas spectral reweighting has a null effect.
    \item \emph{Design-stage comparison with reconstructors:} On 19 development streams, latent prediction holds causal FPR at $0.6\%\text{--}2.4\%$ versus $22.2\%\text{--}24.3\%$ for reconstructors, and at a matched $1\%$ test FPR \emph{Laminar-JEPA} and \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite} catch $61.2\%$ and $56.0\%$ of events versus $21.7\%$ for TimesNet.
    \item \emph{Explicit limits:} Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse} gives geometric bounds whose scalar score reweighting is null; autocorrelation limits extreme-quantile precision on short calibration blocks; an optional channel branch lifts recall on synthetic single-sensor faults ($80.7\%\text{--}96.5\%$) only by raising test FPR from $1.5\%$ to $6.8\%\text{--}19.4\%$; and slow ramps ($L\ge 4C$) and natural sensor failures remain out of reach.
\end{enumerate}
