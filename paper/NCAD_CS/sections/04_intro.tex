\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Spacecraft, power plants, and industrial machines stream readings from dozens of sensors at once \cite{meng2019spacecraft, chalapathy2019deep, cuellar2024explainable, Hundman2018b}. A detector watching this telemetry has two jobs: raise an alarm soon after something breaks, and stay quiet the rest of the time. Faults are rare and seldom labeled, so the detector must learn what normal looks like from healthy history alone. And operators lose trust quickly: a monitor that alarms several times a day on healthy data is soon ignored, and an ignored monitor catches nothing.

\noindent\emph{Why reconstruction detectors raise too many false alarms:} The most common approach trains a network to reproduce the raw sensor signals and flags moments where the copy is poor; TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}, TranAD \cite{tuli2022tranad}, and Anomaly Transformer \cite{xu2022anomaly} follow this recipe. Raw telemetry, however, is noisy in ways unrelated to faults. Sensor jitter, slow thermal drift, and mismatched channel scales mean that summing reconstruction errors over 25--55 channels produces large spikes even on healthy data. With thresholds set on healthy windows by Streaming Peaks-Over-Threshold (SPOT) \cite{siffer2017anomaly}, these detectors still fire during $22\%\text{--}24\%$ of later healthy data under causal reporting. At that alarm rate, a random alarm generator catches $60.0\%$ of fault events, slightly more than TimesNet ($58.8\%$).

\noindent\emph{Why a single anomaly score is not enough:} A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) \cite{lecun2022path, carmona2021neural} sidesteps raw-signal noise. It compresses each window of sensor data into a short summary of 32 numbers and learns to predict the summary of the data that comes next. Errors in this summary space are far better behaved, and nominal false alarms fall sharply. Standard JEPA detectors, though, still add all 32 coordinate errors into one score and compare it against one threshold. If one or two coordinates drift slightly on healthy data, they push that shared threshold up, and a fault that moves the other 30 coordinates goes unnoticed. We call these drifting coordinates \emph{hijacker dials}. Under strictly causal reporting, such single-score detectors reach a Point-F1 (alarm accuracy scored at every time step) of only $0.050\text{--}0.055$ and need $138\text{--}154$ steps to notice a fault.

\noindent\emph{Our approach, one circuit breaker per dial:} We replace the single alarm with a panel of circuit breakers, much like the breaker box in a building, where a short in one room trips one switch regardless of the total load. The network predicts several future horizons, and for each summary coordinate we track how large its error is at each horizon and whether that error drifts steadily in one direction. Each such reading is a \emph{dial} with its own threshold fitted on healthy data, and the system alarm fires when enough dials trip together (a quorum). Dials that wander on healthy data receive wider thresholds, so a hijacker can no longer mask the rest of the panel. Targeting abrupt and intermediate-onset faults, we study two versions (Fig.~\ref{fig:triad_architecture}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite}, our preferred deployment configuration, predicts three horizons in parallel behind a single layer-normalization (LayerNorm) step \cite{ba2016layernormalization} and watches $4\times32=128$ dials under one quorum. Distilled from the larger model, it has $35\%\text{--}52\%$ fewer parameters and runs $2.43\times$ faster.
    \item \emph{Laminar-JEPA}, the full reference variant, watches $7\times32=224$ dials covering four views of each coordinate's error, plus 32 fast onset relays and an optional weight-editing step that damps drifting coordinates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
Design choices were tuned on 33 development streams. The final evidence comes from 118 untouched streams from 9 datasets (of 141 streams from 12 datasets) that played no role in design; checkpoint selection, model adaptation, and threshold calibration each use a separate block of healthy data (Section~\ref{subsec:cohort_repro}). We report two alarm modes: \emph{causal}, where each alarm uses only data available at that moment, and \emph{buffered}, where a short look-back window supports after-the-fact attribution.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{More faults caught at low false-alarm rates:} On the 118 untouched streams, \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite} catches $63.8\%$ of fault events under causal reporting, against $52.5\%$ for a single-score multi-horizon JEPA. This event recall (ER) gain holds on all $9$ datasets (sign-flip $p=0.0039$) and in all $12$ leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) folds of the full archive. Causal Point-F1 rises from $0.076$ to $0.079$ ($p=0.014$) and buffered Point-F1 from $0.143$ to $0.156$ ($p=0.022$), and the panel beats simpler per-coordinate detectors tuned to the same overall alarm rate.
    \item \emph{Where the gains come from:} Controlled ablations on matched backbones show that LayerNorm compresses heavy error tails and that centering the summaries reduces drift, while spectral reweighting brings no measurable benefit.
    \item \emph{Far fewer false alarms than reconstruction:} On 19 development streams, latent prediction keeps the causal false-positive rate (FPR) at $0.6\%\text{--}2.4\%$, against $22.2\%\text{--}24.3\%$ for reconstruction detectors. When every detector is allowed the same $1\%$ test FPR, \emph{Laminar-JEPA} and \emph{Laminar-JEPA-Lite} catch $61.2\%$ and $56.0\%$ of events, against $21.7\%$ for TimesNet.
    \item \emph{Limits:} Proposition~\ref{prop:anti_collapse} bounds how concentrated the horizon errors can be, yet reweighting scores by this quantity does not help. Strong autocorrelation means that 300 effective calibration samples cannot pin down a 99.9th-percentile threshold precisely. An optional single-channel branch lifts recall of injected single-sensor faults to $80.7\%\text{--}96.5\%$ but raises nominal causal FPR from $1.5\%$ to $6.8\%\text{--}19.4\%$. Slow ramps lasting four or more context windows and natural sensor failures remain out of reach.
\end{enumerate}
