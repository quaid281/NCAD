\section{Ablation Studies and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablations}

To isolate the individual contributions of each mathematical and architectural component, we conduct controlled ablation experiments across multiple telemetry regimes.

\subsection{Ablation 1: Physical Regularizer Controls on Fixed Architectures}
\label{subsec:controlled_ablations}
To test whether the proposed physics-inspired regularizers provide measurable benefits over unconstrained latent prediction, Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) reports controlled ablations across all 19 non-GHL benchmark streams spanning six physical domains (Daphnet, MSL, SMAP, Genesis, Metro, and SWAN; principal models evaluated across five seeds and ablation controls across three seeds under the held-out calibration protocol). In each comparison, the neural backbone is held strictly constant while the specific physical loss term or inductive module is disabled:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the spectral entropy regularizer ($\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$) leaves Point-F1 unchanged (three-seed matched pairing: $0.241 \pm 0.014$ vs. $0.244 \pm 0.024$, difference $-0.003$, $95\%$ bootstrap interval $[-0.011, +0.005]$, Wilcoxon $p=0.62$). Both variants maintain PR-AUC around $0.347\text{--}0.348$.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. $\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the second-moment stress loss ($\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$) yields $0.203 \pm 0.059$ Point-F1 compared to $0.208 \pm 0.071$ with the stress loss active (difference $+0.005$, interval $[-0.006, +0.016]$, $p=0.41$). Neither difference exceeds random seed variation ($p = 0.41$).
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. No-Regime Control:} Disabling multi-subspace regime routing ($K_r=1$) yields $0.129 \pm 0.061$ Point-F1 compared to $0.096 \pm 0.026$ with routing active (difference $-0.033$, interval $[-0.060, -0.010]$, $p=0.048$). Both underperform unconstrained TS-JEPA ($0.234 \pm 0.030$ at five seeds, $p=0.013$).
\end{enumerate}
These controlled ablations across the full 19-stream suite demonstrate that the physical regularizers do not produce statistically detectable improvements over unconstrained latent prediction on telemetry.

\subsection{Ablation 2: Auxiliary Input-Channel Gating Variant in High-Dimensional Sensor Arrays}
In high-dimensional telemetry ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults typically originate in a localized subsystem (e.g., a single valve or battery cell). In standard latent pooling:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\text{pool}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K z_k
\end{equation}
the localized anomalous signal is diluted by ambient normal noise across the remaining quiescent channels. The deployed pipeline mitigates this dilution in latent space via the coordinate-saliency gate on predictive residuals (Section~\ref{subsubsec:causal_saliency_gating}); input channels are encoded unweighted. As an auxiliary variant, we separately evaluate the causal input-channel variance gate $\tilde{x}_k = x_k \cdot \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right)$ applied symmetrically at training and scoring time. Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b) compares this variant against uniform input weighting across four multivariate streams.

\paragraph{Causal Leakage and Preprocessing Ablation}
To characterize the input-gating variant without target-dependent leakage, we train four gating regimes under an identical TS-JEPA configuration ($C=256, S=64$, 10 epochs, seed 42, SPOT $q=10^{-3}$ calibrated on held-out nominal telemetry):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shared Context-Derived Gating:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ are computed strictly from historical context and applied symmetrically to both context and target windows, guaranteeing strict causality ($\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$).
    \item \textbf{Target-Only Leaky Control:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ are computed from the suspect target window. This non-causal control artificially amplifies channels that spike within the target window and upper-bounds the benefit obtainable by violating temporal causality.
    \item \textbf{Independent Gating:} Computing $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ for context and $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ for target applies asymmetric coordinate transformations, breaking latent metric alignment.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Pooling (Deployed):} Input channels are encoded unweighted; this regime coincides with the deployed pipeline, in which dimensional drowning is controlled only by the latent coordinate-saliency gate.
\end{enumerate}
The measured regime comparison is reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b). The effect of input-channel gating is stream-dependent in both sign and magnitude: the context-derived gate substantially improves MSL ($0.047 \to 0.227$), where anomalies concentrate in few coordinates against many quiescent ones, but degrades SMAP ($0.280 \to 0.019$), where fault signatures are distributed across channels and variance weighting attenuates informative coordinates. Under macro-averaged Point-F1, uniform input weighting ($0.118$) outperforms the context-derived gate ($0.077$), while the non-causal target-only control is slightly higher ($0.128$); independent context/target gating is weakest ($0.071$). Because the leaky control captures only a small additional margin ($+8.6\%$ relative over causal gating), most of the gating effect is attributable to quiescent-noise suppression rather than target-window statistics. We therefore retain uniform input weighting in the deployed pipeline and report input-channel gating as an auxiliary variant whose benefit depends on the anomaly's cross-channel dispersion profile.

\paragraph{Fault-Type Sensitivity of Channel Gating}
\label{subsec:gate_fault_study}
To characterize per-fault-type behavior, we inject six single-channel fault classes (flatline, stuck sensor, dropout, low-amplitude step, spike control, and mid-variance flatline) into six multivariate streams and evaluate four gating modes. Two findings emerge. First, the context-derived variance gate approximately doubles point recall relative to uniform pooling (macro $0.045$ vs.\ $0.023$) at roughly three times the nominal false-positive rate ($0.069$ vs.\ $0.019$); a robust-MAD variant is marginally stronger ($0.060$ recall). Second, the increase is concentrated on transient signatures, while quiet faults (flatline, stuck sensor, dropout) remain weakly detected under \textit{every} gating regime (point recall $0.02\text{--}0.07$). The blind spot therefore reflects the aggregate latent discrepancy signal on wide telemetry rather than a deficiency of gating alone, and input-channel gating is reported as an evaluated variant with a precision--recall trade-off rather than adopted in the deployed pipeline.

\subsection{Ablation 3: Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Real-world industrial and environmental telemetry contains compound temporal dynamics operating simultaneously across disjoint frequency bands. For instance, in municipal water infrastructure (\texttt{GECCO}), sensors record 24-hour diurnal consumption cycles alongside sudden pressure drops and pipe contaminant inflows across multi-hour horizons.

Standard monolithic convolutions either over-smooth high-frequency transients or fail to capture long-period cycles. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(c), replacing the standard convolutional front-end with the Fourier-domain dyadic shell decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_j = \mathrm{irfft}(\Psi_j \odot \mathrm{rfft}(\mathbf{x})), \quad j \in \{0, 1, \dots, J-1\}
\end{equation}
followed by per-shell causal convolutions and learned scale routing, produces a modest and stream-dependent effect under an otherwise identical \texttt{TS-JEPA} configuration. Across five multivariate streams, the dyadic front-end improves macro-averaged buffered Point-F1 from $0.088$ to $0.131$ ($+49.6\%$ relative, $+0.044$ absolute), with gains concentrated on MSL ($0.002 \to 0.052$) and SMD ($0.000 \to 0.177$), a small regression on SMAP ($0.294 \to 0.261$), and near-zero detection on \texttt{GECCO} under both configurations. Two caveats apply: enabling the block adds approximately $1.6 \times 10^5$ parameters, so part of the gain may reflect capacity rather than frequency separation, and the measured shell overlap (Section~\ref{sec:proposed_method}) means the decomposition does not attain ideal octave isolation. The dyadic front-end is retained as a modest contributor, not the primary driver of the latent-residual conditioning effect.

\subsection{Ablation 4: Threshold Calibration Rules on Nominal Splits}
\label{subsec:ablation_calibration}
The complete calibrator comparison across all 33 streams and all learned architectures is reported in Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} and Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison}; we summarize the two findings most relevant to the ablation suite here. First, simple non-parametric rules match parametric SPOT: on TS-JEPA, a validation-maximum rule attains held-out FPR $0.0524$ versus $0.0517$ for standard SPOT at nearly identical Point-F1. Second, the kurtosis-adaptive multiplier degrades calibration, inflating TS-JEPA held-out FPR from $0.0517$ to $0.1880$ while leaving Point-F1 statistically indistinguishable ($0.1947$ vs.\ $0.1929$). Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} decomposes this failure into extremal clustering, chronological drift, and GPD goodness-of-fit breakdown.

\subsection{Controlled Backbone Comparison: Latent Space vs. Observation Space}
\label{subsec:controlled_backbone}
A foundational hypothesis of this investigation is that predicting dynamics in a contracted latent representation space produces residual distributions that are fundamentally better conditioned for automated extreme-value thresholding than residuals derived from raw observation-space reconstruction or forecasting. However, directly contrasting full-featured JEPA models against existing public baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD) introduces potential architectural confounding from differing receptive fields, encoder depths, normalization schemes, and pooling operations.

To eliminate these confounding factors, Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} evaluates three architectural formulations under a strictly unified \texttt{HybridTCNEncoder} backbone ($C=256, S=64$, latent dimension $d=32$, 3-layer dilated causal TCN with 48 filters, LayerNorm, GELU, and temporal pooling):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Recon:} Autoencoder encoding the full window $X_{t-C:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{(C+S)\times K}$ into $z \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding back to reconstruct the observation space $\hat{X}$. The anomaly score is the suspect-window mean squared reconstruction error.
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Pred:} Forecaster encoding context $X_{t-C:t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ into $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding directly to forecast upcoming observations $\hat{X}_{t:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$. The anomaly score is the raw observation forecasting residual.
    \item \textbf{TS-JEPA (Latent Space):} Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture encoding context to $z_{\text{ctx}}$, predicting the future latent representation $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = g_\phi(z_{\text{ctx}})$, and scoring via predictive discrepancy against target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from an EMA target encoder.
\end{enumerate}
All models are trained for 10 epochs using AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$) with batch size 16 and calibrated via Kurtosis-Adaptive EVT ($u=0.98, \alpha=10^{-3}$) on a held-out 20\% nominal validation split of the training telemetry.

\noindent\textbf{Observation Reconstruction vs. Observation Forecasting:} A central question is whether the reduction in false alarms stems from forecasting upcoming dynamics rather than reconstructing full windows, or whether it requires representation in latent space. The matched-backbone results in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} resolve this question. Comparing TCN-Obs-Recon and TCN-Obs-Pred reveals virtually identical tail statistics and false-positive rates: validation kurtosis is $6.09$ vs.\ $6.00$, test kurtosis is $57.26$ vs.\ $55.66$, and clean empirical FPR is $27.29\%$ vs.\ $27.57\%$. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across the 15 streams confirm that observation reconstruction and observation forecasting are statistically indistinguishable in clean FPR ($p = 0.6784$) and Point-F1 ($p = 1.0000$). Forecasting in raw observation space does not condition residual tails because residuals directly reflect sensor jitter and point-level noise.

\noindent\textbf{Latent Space Projection and Tail Compression:} Shifting the predictive loss to latent representation space (\texttt{TS-JEPA}) fundamentally alters the residual distribution. Test excess kurtosis drops from $55.66$ to $1.77$ (a $>31\times$ reduction in tail weight), producing near-Gaussian residuals. Under held-out EVT calibration, this tail compression significantly lowers the clean empirical false-positive rate from $27.57\%$ to $11.80\%$ (Wilcoxon $p = 0.0479$), eliminating over $6,500$ false alarms per stream. Under severe noise injection ($\text{SNR}=0\text{ dB}$), observation-space models experience a catastrophic failure in threshold calibration, with FPR surging past $50\%$ ($54.10\%$ for reconstruction, $50.98\%$ for forecasting). In contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} maintains an empirical FPR of $13.93\%$ ($p = 0.0026$), confirming that latent representations provide stability against external sensor perturbations.

\noindent\textbf{The Operational Sensitivity Trade-Off:} The tail stability of latent prediction incurs a measurable operational trade-off in point-level detection sensitivity. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone}, clean Point-F1 declines from $0.1916$ for TCN-Obs-Pred to $0.0785$ for TS-JEPA (Wilcoxon $p = 0.0054$), with point recall dropping from $0.4315$ to $0.1460$. Because the latent encoder condenses multi-channel sequences into semantic trajectory vectors, localized point anomalies that do not perturb macroscopic state evolution are filtered alongside noise. Latent prediction thus operates as a conservative state detector, suppressing false alarms at the price of reduced sensitivity to subtle, isolated point faults.

\noindent\textbf{Investigation of Native Window Horizons:} A secondary hypothesis is whether reconstructive baselines suffered under automated EVT calibration because they were evaluated on long temporal windows ($W=320$) rather than their originally proposed native horizons ($W=10$ for TranAD, $W=96$ for TimesNet). To test this, Table~\ref{tab:native_window_baselines} compares each baseline under both native and $W=320$ horizons across five diverse telemetry streams. 

The empirical evidence refutes the window-length hypothesis. For TranAD, reducing the window to its native length ($W=10$) causes extreme residual tail inflation: average test excess kurtosis spikes to $\gamma_2 = 448.25$ (a $15\times$ increase over $W=320$, reaching $\gamma_2 = 809.32$ on GECCO and $781.44$ on SMAP). Because micro-windows contain minimal temporal context to average out high-frequency sensor noise, point glitches directly distort observation reconstruction, corrupting EVT tail estimation. Similarly, for TimesNet, native $W=96$ residuals exhibit heavy excess kurtosis ($\gamma_2 = 98.14$) and an average empirical false positive rate of $44.09\%$, generating over $26,500$ false alarms per stream. By contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} maintains well-conditioned residuals ($\gamma_2 = 3.14$) and achieves the lowest empirical false positive rate ($22.57\%$). Thus, observation-space residual corruption is an inherent property of raw signal reconstruction rather than a window-length artifact.
