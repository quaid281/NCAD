\section{Ablation Studies and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablations}

To isolate the individual contributions of each mathematical and architectural component, we conduct controlled ablation experiments across multiple telemetry regimes.

\subsection{Ablation 1: Physical Regularizer Controls on Fixed Architectures}
\label{subsec:controlled_ablations}
To test whether the proposed physics-inspired regularizers provide measurable benefits over unconstrained latent prediction, Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(a) reports controlled ablations on a multi-seed subset of seven benchmark streams (SMAP \texttt{P-3}, \texttt{A-1}; MSL \texttt{M-1}, \texttt{C-1}; SMD \texttt{machine-1-2}, \texttt{machine-1-3}; Daphnet \texttt{S01R01E1}; evaluated across 3 seeds under the held-out calibration protocol). In each comparison, the neural backbone is held strictly constant while the specific physical loss term or inductive module is disabled:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator-Entropy vs. $\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the spectral entropy regularizer ($\lambda_{\text{ent}}=0$) leaves Point-F1 unchanged ($0.018 \pm 0.019$ vs. $0.017 \pm 0.015$, difference $+0.001$, $95\%$ bootstrap interval $[-0.006, +0.009]$, Wilcoxon $p=1.00$). Both variants maintain PR-AUC around $0.155\text{--}0.156$.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Stress vs. $\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$ Control:} Disabling the second-moment stress loss ($\alpha_{\text{stress}}=0$) yields $0.033 \pm 0.035$ Point-F1 compared to $0.017 \pm 0.012$ with the stress loss active. Scoring via the mean predictive discrepancy alone yields $0.040 \pm 0.022$. Neither difference exceeds random seed variation ($p \ge 0.375$).
    \item \textbf{Potential-Flow vs. No-Regime Control:} Disabling multi-subspace regime routing ($K_r=1$) yields $0.068 \pm 0.082$ Point-F1 compared to $0.074 \pm 0.069$ with routing active (difference $+0.006$, interval $[-0.022, +0.032]$, $p=0.58$).
\end{enumerate}
These controlled ablations demonstrate that the physical regularizers do not produce statistically detectable improvements over unconstrained latent prediction on telemetry.

\subsection{Ablation 2: Coordinate Saliency Gating in High-Dimensional Sensor Arrays}
In high-dimensional telemetry ($K \ge 25$), anomalous faults typically originate in a localized subsystem (e.g., a single valve or battery cell). In standard latent pooling:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\text{pool}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K z_k
\end{equation}
the localized anomalous signal is diluted by ambient normal noise across the remaining quiescent channels. 

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(b), disabling Coordinate Saliency Gating ($\tilde{x}_k = x_k$) causes a substantial drop in Point-F1 on MSL ($0.2439 \to 0.1741$) and SMAP ($0.4157 \to 0.1982$). The parameter-free saliency gate $\tilde{x}_k = x_k \cdot \sigma\left(\frac{s_k - \bar{s}}{\sigma_s + \epsilon}\right)$ dynamically isolates high-variance, non-stationary coordinates, suppressing ambient quiescent dimensions and maintaining high signal-to-noise ratio.

\paragraph{Causal Leakage and Preprocessing Ablation}
To confirm that channel gating does not introduce target-dependent leakage, we evaluate four gating regimes on NASA SMAP ($K=25$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shared Context-Derived Gating (Ours):} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ are computed strictly from historical context and applied symmetrically to both context and target windows. This protocol guarantees strict causality ($\partial \mathbf{g} / \partial \mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \equiv \mathbf{0}$) and achieves \textbf{0.4157} Point-F1.
    \item \textbf{Target-Only Leaky Control:} Gating weights $\mathbf{g} = g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ are computed from the suspect target window. This non-causal control artificially amplifies channels that spike within the target window, achieving $0.4285$ Point-F1 ($+3.1\%$ relative over causal gating), demonstrating that context-derived gating captures virtually all of the anti-drowning benefit without violating temporal causality.
    \item \textbf{Independent Gating:} Computing $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{ctx}})$ for context and $g(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}})$ for target applies asymmetric coordinate transformations, breaking latent metric alignment and reducing Point-F1 to $0.3340$.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Pooling (No Gating):} Disabling channel weighting completely yields $0.1982$ Point-F1 (a $52.3\%$ relative degradation).
\end{enumerate}
These comparisons confirm that the performance gain stems from suppressing quiescent noise rather than leaking target-window statistics.

\subsection{Ablation 3: Littlewood-Paley Dyadic Wavelet Shells}
Real-world industrial and environmental telemetry contains compound temporal dynamics operating simultaneously across disjoint frequency bands. For instance, in municipal water infrastructure (\texttt{GECCO}), sensors record 24-hour diurnal consumption cycles alongside sudden pressure drops and pipe contaminant inflows across multi-hour horizons.

Standard monolithic convolutions either over-smooth high-frequency transients or fail to capture long-period cycles. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(c), replacing standard convolutions with Littlewood-Paley dyadic wavelet projections:
\begin{equation}
    z^{(j)} = \Psi_j \ast x, \quad j \in \{0, 1, \dots, J-1\}
\end{equation}
improves Point-F1 on \texttt{GECCO} from $0.1429$ to $0.4040$ on \texttt{Operator-Entropy JEPA}, while lifting Point-F1 on \texttt{SWAN} magnetograms from $0.2210$ to $0.4349$ and on \texttt{Daphnet} accelerometry from $0.0521$ to $0.0952$. Decoupling frequency octaves prevents low-frequency power from masking subtle high-frequency phase disruptions.

\subsection{Ablation 4: Threshold Calibration Rules on Nominal Splits}
\label{subsec:ablation_calibration}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations}(d) isolates the effect of different calibration rules on held-out nominal telemetry residuals for TS-JEPA and Operator-Entropy JEPA. While standard SPOT ($q=10^{-3}$) achieves a nominal FPR of $0.052$ on TS-JEPA, simple non-parametric alternatives achieve comparable performance: a 99.5th percentile rule yields an FPR of $0.066$ ($0.245$ Point-F1), and taking the validation maximum yields $0.052$ ($0.248$ Point-F1). Modulating the threshold by sample excess kurtosis ($\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau(1 + \frac{1}{6}\tanh\gamma_2)$) increases held-out FPR from $0.052$ to $0.188$ on TS-JEPA without improving Point-F1 ($0.231$ vs.\ $0.254$). Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_realities} and Table~\ref{tab:calibrator_comparison} provide the complete 33-stream benchmark evaluating these calibration behaviors across all learned architectures.

\subsection{Controlled Backbone Comparison: Latent Space vs. Observation Space}
A foundational hypothesis of this investigation is that predicting dynamics in a contracted latent representation space produces residual distributions that are fundamentally better conditioned for automated extreme-value thresholding than residuals derived from raw observation-space reconstruction or forecasting. However, directly contrasting full-featured JEPA models against existing public baselines (e.g., TimesNet, TranAD) introduces potential architectural confounding from differing receptive fields, encoder depths, normalization schemes, and pooling operations.

To eliminate these confounding factors, Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone} evaluates three architectural formulations under a strictly unified \texttt{HybridTCNEncoder} backbone ($C=256, S=64$, latent dimension $d=32$, 3-layer dilated causal TCN with 48 filters, LayerNorm, GELU, and temporal pooling):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Recon:} Autoencoder encoding the full window $X_{t-C:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{(C+S)\times K}$ into $z \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding back to reconstruct the observation space $\hat{X}$. The anomaly score is the suspect-window mean squared reconstruction error.
    \item \textbf{TCN-Obs-Pred:} Forecaster encoding context $X_{t-C:t} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times K}$ into $z_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ and decoding directly to forecast upcoming observations $\hat{X}_{t:t+S} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times K}$. The anomaly score is the raw observation forecasting residual.
    \item \textbf{TS-JEPA (Latent Space):} Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture encoding context to $z_{\text{ctx}}$, predicting the future latent representation $\hat{z}_{\text{tgt}} = g_\phi(z_{\text{ctx}})$, and scoring via predictive discrepancy against target representation $z_{\text{tgt}}$ from an EMA target encoder.
\end{enumerate}
All models are trained for 10 epochs using AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$) with batch size 16 and calibrated via Kurtosis-Adaptive EVT ($u=0.98, \alpha=10^{-3}$) on a held-out 20\% nominal validation split of the training telemetry.

\noindent\textbf{Tail Conditioning and False Alarm Suppression:} As reported in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_backbone}, observation-space models exhibit severe excess kurtosis across the fifteen benchmark streams ($\gamma_2 = 55.66\text{--}57.26$), driven by point-level measurement jitter and localized sensor noise. This heavy-tail inflation forces EVT thresholding into substantial miscalibration, yielding empirical false positive rates of $27.3\%\text{--}27.6\%$ and generating over $10,400$ false alarms per stream. Conversely, \texttt{TS-JEPA} projects telemetry into a normalized latent manifold, compressing high-frequency observation noise and yielding near-Gaussian test residuals ($\gamma_2 = 1.77$, a $>32\times$ reduction in tail weight). Consequently, EVT establishes tight, stable thresholds, reducing the empirical false positive rate to $11.80\%$ and eliminating over $6,500$ false alarms per stream (a $62.6\%$ reduction).

\noindent\textbf{Noise-Injection Robustness:} Under severe additive jitter ($\text{SNR}=0\text{ dB}$), observation-space residuals experience drastic variance inflation: empirical FPR nearly doubles for TCN-Obs-Recon ($27.29\% \to 54.10\%$, $+26.8$ percentage points). In stark contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} empirical FPR shifts by only $2.1$ percentage points ($11.80\% \to 13.94\%$), proving that latent joint-embedding architectures inherently filter high-frequency sensor perturbations across multi-domain physical telemetry.

\noindent\textbf{Investigation of Native Window Horizons:} A secondary hypothesis is whether reconstructive baselines suffered under automated EVT calibration because they were evaluated on long temporal windows ($W=320$) rather than their originally proposed native horizons ($W=10$ for TranAD, $W=96$ for TimesNet). To test this, Table~\ref{tab:native_window_baselines} compares each baseline under both native and $W=320$ horizons across five diverse telemetry streams. 

The empirical evidence refutes the window-length hypothesis. For TranAD, reducing the window to its native length ($W=10$) causes extreme residual tail inflation: average test excess kurtosis spikes to $\gamma_2 = 448.25$ (a $15\times$ increase over $W=320$, reaching $\gamma_2 = 809.32$ on GECCO and $781.44$ on SMAP). Because micro-windows contain minimal temporal context to average out high-frequency sensor noise, point glitches directly distort observation reconstruction, corrupting EVT tail estimation. Similarly, for TimesNet, native $W=96$ residuals exhibit heavy excess kurtosis ($\gamma_2 = 98.14$) and an average empirical false positive rate of $44.09\%$, generating over $26,500$ false alarms per stream. By contrast, \texttt{TS-JEPA} maintains well-conditioned residuals ($\gamma_2 = 3.14$) and achieves the lowest empirical false positive rate ($22.57\%$). Thus, observation-space residual corruption is an inherent property of raw signal reconstruction rather than a window-length artifact.
