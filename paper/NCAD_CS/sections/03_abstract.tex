\begin{abstract}
Automated anomaly thresholds can generate excessive alarms when telemetry scores change between calibration and deployment. We compare latent predictive, observation-space, and simple bounded scores under chronological held-out calibration on 33 streams from seven datasets; a 19-stream non-GHL cohort supports the five-seed controls. On that cohort, the latent predictor has lower nominal false-positive rates than two observation-space reconstruction baselines, but a bounded, dimensionality-matched principal-component control and a bounded observation-space TCN control also produce light residual tails. The TCN control nevertheless has higher calibration false-positive rates, showing that tail shape and threshold transfer are distinct. Causal event-recall curves suggest a different ordering when plotted against realized false-positive rate, but paired interpolation on a common observed range covers few streams and does not establish an aggregate advantage; buffered attribution is analyzed separately. Point-F1 and precision-recall differences remain imprecise, not equivalent. Static peaks-over-threshold calibration substantially overshoots its target risk under this short-block protocol. Inter-block score drift and scarce tail exceedances are both plausible contributors; their relative causal roles remain unresolved. Three physics-inspired variants show no reliable aggregate detection gain. These findings emphasize matched score controls, reporting of realized alarm rates, and limits on what nominal-budget curves can establish.
\end{abstract}
